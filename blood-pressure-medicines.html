Blood Pressure Medicines

Understanding common medicines used to control blood pressure, how they work, important side effects, interactions, and safe use.

What Are Blood Pressure Medicines?

Blood pressure medicines, also called antihypertensive medicines, help lower blood pressure and reduce the risk of complications such as stroke, heart disease, heart failure, and kidney disease.

Different medicines lower blood pressure through different mechanisms. Some people need one medicine, while others may require a combination of medicines.

Important:

The choice of medicine depends on blood pressure, age, kidney function, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, pregnancy status, other conditions, and other medicines being taken.

ACE Inhibitors

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors reduce the formation of angiotensin II, a hormone that contributes to blood-vessel narrowing and sodium retention.

Examples

  • Enalapril
  • Lisinopril
  • Ramipril
  • Captopril

Important adverse effects

  • Dry cough
  • Increased potassium
  • Changes in kidney function
  • Angioedema
Important:

ACE inhibitors should not be used during pregnancy. Kidney function and potassium may need monitoring.

Angiotensin Receptor Blockers

ARBs block the effects of angiotensin II and can lower blood pressure while providing cardiovascular and kidney-related benefits in selected patients.

Examples

  • Losartan
  • Valsartan
  • Irbesartan
  • Candesartan

ARBs are often considered when an ACE inhibitor causes troublesome cough.

Safety:

ARBs can increase potassium and affect kidney function. They should not be used during pregnancy.

Calcium Channel Blockers

Calcium channel blockers reduce blood-vessel contraction and lower blood pressure. Some also affect heart rate and conduction.

Examples

  • Amlodipine
  • Nifedipine
  • Diltiazem
  • Verapamil

Possible adverse effects

  • Ankle swelling
  • Headache
  • Flushing
  • Dizziness
  • Constipation, particularly with verapamil
  • Slow heart rate with some agents

Thiazide & Thiazide-Like Diuretics

These medicines increase the excretion of sodium and water by the kidneys and help reduce blood pressure.

Examples

  • Hydrochlorothiazide
  • Chlorthalidone
  • Indapamide

Important considerations

  • Low sodium
  • Low potassium
  • Increased uric acid
  • Changes in glucose or lipid levels in some patients
  • Dehydration

Beta-Blockers

Beta-blockers reduce sympathetic effects on the cardiovascular system, lowering heart rate and, for some agents, blood pressure.

Examples

  • Metoprolol
  • Bisoprolol
  • Atenolol
  • Carvedilol

Beta-blockers are especially important when hypertension occurs with particular cardiovascular conditions such as heart failure, previous myocardial infarction, or certain rhythm disorders.

Do not stop suddenly:

Abrupt discontinuation of some beta-blockers can cause problems such as increased heart rate, blood pressure, or worsening angina.

Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists

Medicines such as spironolactone and eplerenone block aldosterone effects and can be used in selected patients with resistant hypertension and certain cardiovascular conditions.

Important risks

  • Increased potassium
  • Changes in kidney function
  • Breast tenderness or gynecomastia with spironolactone

Why Are Multiple Medicines Sometimes Used?

Blood pressure is controlled through several physiological systems. Combining medicines from different classes can therefore provide greater blood-pressure reduction while allowing different mechanisms to work together.

Combination therapy may also be appropriate when blood pressure remains above the individual's treatment target despite treatment with one medicine.

Remember:

More medicines do not automatically mean better treatment. The regimen should be individualized and monitored for benefit and adverse effects.

Important Drug Interactions

ACE inhibitors / ARBs + potassium

These medicines can increase potassium. Combining them with potassium supplements or other medicines that raise potassium can increase the risk of hyperkalemia.

ACE inhibitors / ARBs + NSAIDs

NSAIDs can affect kidney function and may reduce the blood-pressure-lowering effect of some medicines. The combination requires caution, particularly when a diuretic is also being used.

Beta-blockers + medicines affecting heart rate

Combining beta-blockers with certain medicines such as verapamil or diltiazem can excessively slow heart rate or conduction.

Diuretics + other medicines

Diuretics can alter electrolytes and kidney function, which may influence the safety of other medicines.

Kidney Function

Kidney function is important when selecting and monitoring many blood-pressure medicines.

ACE inhibitors and ARBs can cause changes in creatinine and potassium, while diuretics can affect fluid balance and electrolytes.

Patients with chronic kidney disease may have specific treatment considerations and require regular monitoring.

Pregnancy

Some blood-pressure medicines are unsuitable during pregnancy.

ACE inhibitors and ARBs should not be used during pregnancy because of risks to the fetus.

Pregnant patients with hypertension require individualized treatment from a qualified healthcare professional.

Taking Blood Pressure Medicines at Home

  • Take medicines according to the prescribed schedule.
  • Do not stop treatment simply because blood pressure has improved.
  • Monitor blood pressure when advised.
  • Keep a record of blood-pressure readings when useful.
  • Attend follow-up appointments.
  • Report troublesome dizziness, fainting, swelling, persistent cough, or other concerning symptoms.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about OTC medicines, herbal products, and supplements.

Medicines & Lifestyle

Blood-pressure medicines work together with healthy lifestyle habits.

  • Reduce excess dietary sodium.
  • Maintain appropriate physical activity.
  • Avoid tobacco.
  • Limit alcohol where applicable.
  • Maintain a healthy weight when appropriate.
  • Follow a balanced diet.

When to Seek Urgent Care

Very high blood pressure accompanied by symptoms suggesting acute organ injury requires urgent medical assessment.

  • Chest pain
  • Severe shortness of breath
  • New weakness or numbness
  • Difficulty speaking
  • Severe confusion
  • Sudden vision changes
  • Severe headache with concerning neurological symptoms

Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Safe antihypertensive therapy requires attention to:

  • Blood-pressure response
  • Adherence
  • Kidney function
  • Potassium and other electrolytes
  • Drug interactions
  • Adverse effects
  • Diabetes and cardiovascular disease
  • Pregnancy status when relevant

Educational Information

This page provides general educational information about blood-pressure medicines. It does not replace individualized medical assessment or prescribing advice.

Medicine selection and treatment targets should be determined according to the patient's clinical condition and current clinical guidance.

Control Blood Pressure

Consistent treatment, monitoring, and healthy lifestyle habits help reduce the long-term risks associated with hypertension.

Know Your Medicines

Learn the purpose of each medicine and discuss adverse effects or concerns with a healthcare professional.

Monitoring Matters

Some antihypertensive medicines require monitoring of kidney function, potassium, electrolytes, blood pressure, or heart rate.
