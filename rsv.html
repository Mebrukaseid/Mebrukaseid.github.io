RESPIRATORY INFECTION

RSV Infection

Understanding respiratory syncytial virus, its symptoms, complications, treatment, prevention, and medicine safety.

What is RSV?

Respiratory syncytial virus, commonly called RSV, is a common virus that infects the respiratory tract, including the nose, throat, and lungs.

RSV can cause mild cold-like illness in many people. However, it can sometimes cause more serious lower respiratory tract infections such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia.

Almost all children experience an RSV infection during the first few years of life, but the severity of illness can vary considerably.

Common symptoms

RSV symptoms often develop gradually and may include:

  • Runny nose
  • Nasal congestion
  • Sneezing
  • Cough
  • Fever
  • Reduced appetite
  • Wheezing
  • Tiredness or decreased activity

In very young infants, RSV may present differently. Irritability, decreased activity, poor feeding, and pauses in breathing can occur.

How does RSV spread?

RSV spreads through infectious respiratory particles and through contact with infected respiratory secretions.

Infection can occur when a person comes into close contact with someone who has RSV or touches contaminated surfaces and then touches the eyes, nose, or mouth.

Reducing transmission

  • Wash hands regularly with soap and water.
  • Avoid close contact with people who are sick.
  • Avoid touching the eyes, nose, and mouth with unwashed hands.
  • Clean frequently touched surfaces.
  • Improve ventilation in indoor spaces.
  • Keep sick children and adults away from people at higher risk when possible.

Who is at higher risk of severe RSV?

RSV can become more serious in certain groups, especially:

  • Very young infants, particularly infants under 6 months of age.
  • Premature infants.
  • Children with chronic lung disease.
  • Children with certain congenital heart diseases.
  • Children with weakened immune systems.
  • Older adults.
  • Adults with chronic heart or lung disease.
  • People with weakened immune systems.
Important:

RSV can worsen existing conditions such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and heart failure.

Possible complications

Most RSV infections are mild, but severe infection can affect the lower respiratory tract.

  • Bronchiolitis
  • Pneumonia
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Low oxygen levels
  • Dehydration
  • Worsening of chronic lung disease
  • Worsening of chronic heart disease

Diagnosis

RSV can often be suspected based on symptoms and clinical assessment.

Laboratory testing may be used when knowing the specific respiratory virus would affect clinical management, infection control, or public health decisions.

Testing methods can include molecular tests and antigen-based tests.

Treatment

There is no routinely recommended specific antiviral treatment for uncomplicated RSV infection. Most infections improve on their own.

Supportive care

Treatment mainly focuses on relieving symptoms and maintaining adequate hydration and breathing.

  • Adequate fluid intake.
  • Appropriate fever and pain management.
  • Nasal care to help clear secretions.
  • Monitoring feeding and hydration in infants.
  • Oxygen therapy when clinically indicated.

Severe RSV infection

People with severe RSV may require hospital care. Treatment can include oxygen therapy, hydration, nutritional support, and close monitoring.

Important:

Antibiotics do not treat RSV because RSV is a viral infection. Antibiotics may only be needed when a healthcare professional identifies a bacterial infection or complication.

Home care

Maintain hydration

Encourage adequate fluid intake. Infants should continue appropriate breast milk or formula feeding. Small, frequent feeds may be easier when a child is congested.

Clear nasal secretions

Saline nasal drops and gentle nasal suction can help relieve nasal congestion in infants when needed.

Rest

Adequate rest can support recovery.

Avoid smoke

Keep children and adults with respiratory infections away from cigarette smoke and other respiratory irritants.

Monitor breathing

Pay close attention to breathing, feeding, activity, and hydration, particularly in young infants.

For infants:

A child who is drinking much less than usual, producing fewer wet diapers, becoming unusually sleepy, or having difficulty breathing should be assessed promptly.

Medicine safety

Extra care is needed when using medicines for young children and older adults.

  • Do not give aspirin to children because of the risk of serious adverse effects.
  • Check children's medicines carefully because some products are not appropriate for young children.
  • Avoid combining multiple cold medicines without checking their active ingredients.
  • Ask a healthcare professional before giving non-prescription medicines to infants.
  • Older adults should check whether non-prescription medicines can increase blood pressure or interact with regular medicines.
  • Antibiotics should not be used unnecessarily for viral infections.

Prevention

Prevention is particularly important for infants and people at increased risk of severe RSV disease.

  • Wash hands frequently.
  • Avoid close contact with people who are sick.
  • Keep sick people away from high-risk infants when possible.
  • Clean frequently touched surfaces.
  • Avoid exposing infants to tobacco smoke.
  • Follow locally available RSV immunization recommendations for eligible people.

RSV immunization

Current RSV prevention options include maternal vaccination during pregnancy and long-acting monoclonal antibodies for eligible infants. Vaccines are also available for certain older adults and adults at increased risk of severe disease.

Important:

RSV immunization recommendations vary by country, age, pregnancy status, risk factors, and available products. Eligibility should be confirmed with a qualified healthcare professional or local health authority.

When to seek urgent medical care

Seek urgent medical attention when serious symptoms develop.

  • Difficulty breathing.
  • Rapid or labored breathing.
  • Pauses in breathing in an infant.
  • Bluish or unusually pale lips or skin.
  • Difficulty drinking or feeding.
  • Signs of dehydration.
  • Severe weakness or unusual sleepiness.
  • Worsening symptoms.
Do not wait:

Breathing difficulty, repeated pauses in breathing, poor fluid intake, or signs of dehydration in an infant require prompt medical assessment.

Educational information

This page provides general health education and is not a substitute for diagnosis, prescription, individualized treatment, or professional medical advice.

Treatment and prevention recommendations may change as evidence, vaccines, medicines, and public health guidance develop. Always follow current advice from qualified healthcare professionals and relevant health authorities.

Protect vulnerable people

RSV can become serious in young infants, older adults, and people with certain medical conditions. Pay particular attention to breathing and hydration.
