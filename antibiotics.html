MEDICINES

Antibiotics

Understanding antibiotic classes, common uses, safety, side effects, interactions, and responsible antibiotic use.

What Are Antibiotics?

Antibiotics are medicines used to treat certain infections caused by bacteria. They work by killing bacteria or preventing them from growing.

Antibiotics do not treat viral infections such as the common cold or influenza. Using antibiotics when they are not needed provides no benefit and can still cause adverse effects and contribute to antibiotic resistance.

Key point:

Antibiotics should be used when there is an appropriate clinical indication and selected according to the suspected or confirmed infection, patient factors, and local guidance.

Major Antibiotic Classes

Antibiotics belong to different pharmacological classes. Each class has characteristic mechanisms, indications, adverse effects, and precautions.

Penicillins

Examples include amoxicillin, ampicillin, penicillin G, and flucloxacillin. Some penicillins are combined with beta-lactamase inhibitors, such as amoxicillin with clavulanic acid.

Cephalosporins

Examples include cefalexin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and cefepime. Their antibacterial spectrum differs between generations and individual medicines.

Macrolides

Examples include azithromycin, clarithromycin, and erythromycin. They inhibit bacterial protein synthesis.

Tetracyclines

Doxycycline is a commonly used tetracycline. Tetracyclines have important precautions in some populations and interactions with minerals such as calcium, iron, and magnesium.

Aminoglycosides

Examples include gentamicin and amikacin. They are important medicines for selected serious bacterial infections but require careful monitoring because of potential kidney and hearing toxicity.

Fluoroquinolones

Examples include ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and moxifloxacin. Their use should be based on appropriate indications because of important adverse effects and antimicrobial stewardship considerations.

Sulfonamides

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is an important combination used for selected bacterial infections. It has several clinically important interactions and precautions.

Other important antibiotics

Other classes include glycopeptides, lincosamides, oxazolidinones, carbapenems, and several other antibacterial groups used according to the type and severity of infection.

How Do Antibiotics Work?

Different antibiotics act on different bacterial structures or processes.

  • Some inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis.
  • Some inhibit bacterial protein synthesis.
  • Some interfere with bacterial DNA or RNA processes.
  • Some interfere with important bacterial metabolic pathways.
  • Some damage the bacterial cell membrane.

When Are Antibiotics Used?

Antibiotics may be appropriate for bacterial infections such as selected urinary tract, respiratory, skin, bloodstream, gastrointestinal, and other bacterial infections.

The appropriate antibiotic depends on factors such as the suspected organism, infection site, severity, local resistance patterns, allergies, kidney and liver function, age, pregnancy status, and other medicines.

Not every infection needs an antibiotic.

Many respiratory infections are caused by viruses, against which antibiotics are ineffective.

Common Side Effects

Antibiotics can cause adverse effects even when they are appropriately prescribed.

  • Nausea
  • Diarrhea
  • Abdominal discomfort
  • Skin rash
  • Dizziness
  • Fungal or yeast infections

Some antibiotics can cause serious adverse reactions, including severe allergic reactions and antibiotic- associated Clostridioides difficile infection.

Antibiotic Allergy

Some people experience allergic reactions to antibiotics. Reactions can range from mild skin symptoms to life-threatening anaphylaxis.

Emergency symptoms:

Difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, severe dizziness, fainting, or rapidly developing widespread allergic symptoms require emergency medical attention.

A reported antibiotic allergy should be discussed with a healthcare professional because the exact medicine and reaction history can affect future treatment choices.

Important Antibiotic Interactions

Some antibiotics interact with other medicines, foods, minerals, or supplements.

Warfarin

Several antibiotics can affect anticoagulation and may require closer monitoring when used with warfarin.

Antacids and minerals

Some antibiotics, particularly tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones, can bind minerals such as calcium, iron, magnesium, or aluminum and reduce absorption.

QT-prolonging medicines

Some antibiotics, including certain macrolides and fluoroquinolones, can contribute to QT prolongation. The risk can increase when combined with other QT-prolonging medicines.

Other medicines

Some antibiotics affect drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters and can change the concentration of other medicines.

Kidney & Liver Function

Kidney and liver function can influence antibiotic selection, dosing, or monitoring.

Some antibiotics are primarily eliminated through the kidneys, while others undergo substantial hepatic metabolism. Patients with kidney or liver impairment may therefore require individualized treatment decisions.

Important:

Antibiotic doses should not be changed based only on information from a general website. Individual renal and hepatic function should be assessed by a healthcare professional.

Pregnancy & Special Populations

Antibiotic selection may be different during pregnancy, breastfeeding, childhood, older age, or when significant kidney or liver disease is present.

Not all antibiotics have the same safety profile in these populations. Treatment should therefore be selected by a qualified healthcare professional based on the infection and patient characteristics.

Antibiotic Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when microorganisms become able to survive medicines that previously treated them effectively.

Misuse and overuse of antibiotics are important drivers of antimicrobial resistance. Resistant infections can become more difficult to treat and may require alternative medicines or longer treatment.

Protect antibiotics:

Use antibiotics only when they are needed, follow the prescribed treatment instructions, and never share antibiotics with another person.

Safe Antibiotic Use

  • Use antibiotics only when prescribed or otherwise appropriately recommended by a qualified healthcare professional.
  • Take the medicine according to the prescribed instructions.
  • Do not share antibiotics with other people.
  • Do not use leftover antibiotics for a future illness.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about previous antibiotic allergies or serious reactions.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about all other medicines, supplements, and herbal products.
  • Ask what to do if a dose is missed or an adverse effect develops.

When to Seek Medical Help

Seek urgent medical attention if a person taking an antibiotic develops serious symptoms such as:

  • Difficulty breathing
  • Swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat
  • Fainting or severe dizziness
  • Severe or persistent diarrhea
  • Severe skin reactions
  • Rapid worsening of the infection

Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Appropriate antibiotic use involves more than choosing an antibacterial medicine. Important considerations include:

  • Correct indication
  • Appropriate antibiotic selection
  • Allergy assessment
  • Renal and hepatic function
  • Drug interactions
  • Adverse-effect monitoring
  • Antimicrobial susceptibility when appropriate
  • Antimicrobial stewardship

These principles help maximize treatment benefit while reducing avoidable harm and unnecessary antibiotic resistance.

Educational Information

This page provides general educational information about antibiotics. It does not replace diagnosis, prescribing, or individualized medical advice.

Antibiotic choice depends on the infection, patient characteristics, local resistance patterns, and current clinical guidance.

Antibiotics Are Not Always Needed

Antibiotics treat certain bacterial infections, but they do not treat viral infections such as most colds and influenza.

Fight Resistance

Appropriate antibiotic use helps protect the effectiveness of these important medicines for current and future patients.

Medicine Safety

Always consider allergies, interactions, kidney and liver function, pregnancy, age, and other patient-specific factors.
