CHRONIC DISEASE

Hypertension

Understanding high blood pressure, its risk factors, complications, treatment, medicines, and lifestyle care.

What is hypertension?

Hypertension, commonly called high blood pressure, is a condition in which blood pressure remains higher than the recommended level over time.

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of blood vessels. It is expressed using two numbers: systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure.

Hypertension often develops without noticeable symptoms. This is why regular blood pressure measurement is important, even when a person feels healthy.

Understanding blood pressure

A blood pressure measurement contains two values.

Systolic blood pressure

The systolic value is the pressure in the arteries when the heart contracts and pumps blood.

Diastolic blood pressure

The diastolic value is the pressure in the arteries when the heart relaxes between beats.

Important:

A single elevated blood pressure reading does not always establish a diagnosis of hypertension. Blood pressure should be assessed using appropriate repeated measurements and clinical evaluation.

Symptoms

Hypertension usually does not cause obvious symptoms. Many people can have high blood pressure for years without knowing it.

Very high blood pressure can sometimes be associated with symptoms such as:

  • Severe headache
  • Visual changes
  • Chest discomfort
  • Shortness of breath
  • Confusion
  • Neurological symptoms
Remember:

Feeling well does not mean blood pressure is normal. Regular measurement is the reliable way to detect hypertension.

Risk factors

Several factors can increase the risk of developing hypertension.

  • Increasing age
  • Family history of hypertension
  • High salt intake
  • Overweight or obesity
  • Physical inactivity
  • Tobacco use
  • Excessive alcohol consumption
  • Unhealthy dietary patterns
  • Diabetes
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Sleep-related breathing disorders

Why is hypertension important?

Long-term uncontrolled hypertension can damage blood vessels and important organs.

  • Heart disease
  • Heart failure
  • Stroke
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Damage to blood vessels
  • Vision problems
  • Increased cardiovascular risk

Effective blood pressure control can reduce the risk of many of these complications.

Diagnosis and monitoring

Hypertension is diagnosed using blood pressure measurements obtained correctly and interpreted in the context of the patient's overall health.

Proper measurement matters

  • Sit quietly before measurement.
  • Use an appropriately sized blood pressure cuff.
  • Keep the arm supported.
  • Keep the feet supported and avoid talking during the measurement.
  • Take repeated measurements when appropriate.

Additional assessment

A healthcare professional may assess cardiovascular risk and look for conditions that can contribute to hypertension or complications from high blood pressure.

Depending on the situation, this may include blood tests, urine tests, kidney assessment, blood glucose evaluation, lipid testing, and other investigations.

Treatment

Treatment aims to reduce blood pressure and lower the risk of cardiovascular, kidney, and other complications.

Management commonly combines lifestyle changes with medicines when medication is indicated.

Lifestyle measures

  • Reduce excessive dietary salt.
  • Eat a balanced diet rich in vegetables, fruits, and other nutritious foods.
  • Maintain a healthy body weight.
  • Engage in regular physical activity when medically appropriate.
  • Avoid tobacco.
  • Limit excessive alcohol consumption.
  • Maintain adequate sleep.

Medicines

Several groups of medicines can be used to treat hypertension. Selection depends on blood pressure level, cardiovascular risk, kidney function, diabetes, age, pregnancy status, other diseases, and other medicines being taken.

  • ACE inhibitors
  • Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)
  • Calcium channel blockers
  • Thiazide-type or thiazide-like diuretics
  • Other medicines in selected situations
Important:

The choice of blood pressure medicine should be individualized by a qualified healthcare professional. Do not start, stop, or change a blood pressure medicine without appropriate advice.

Understanding Blood Pressure Medicines

Different blood pressure medicines lower blood pressure in different ways. The choice depends on blood pressure level, age, kidney function, other medical conditions, pregnancy status, other medicines, and the person's cardiovascular risk.

1. ACE inhibitors

Examples include enalapril, lisinopril, and ramipril. These medicines reduce the formation of angiotensin II, a substance that causes blood vessels to constrict. This leads to relaxation of blood vessels and lower blood pressure.

Important adverse effects: dry cough, increased potassium, dizziness, low blood pressure, and changes in kidney function. Rarely, serious angioedema can occur.

Monitoring: blood pressure, serum potassium, and kidney function.

When may treatment need to change?

Significant potassium elevation, significant deterioration in kidney function, symptomatic low blood pressure, or angioedema may require dose reduction or discontinuation. ACE inhibitors should not be used during pregnancy.

2. Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)

Examples include losartan, valsartan, and telmisartan. ARBs block the action of angiotensin II at its receptor, causing blood vessels to relax and reducing blood pressure.

ARBs have similar blood-pressure effects to ACE inhibitors but are much less likely to cause the persistent cough associated with ACE inhibitors.

Important adverse effects: increased potassium, low blood pressure, and changes in kidney function.

Monitoring: blood pressure, potassium, and kidney function.

Important:

ACE inhibitors and ARBs should generally not be used together for hypertension. ARBs, like ACE inhibitors, should not be used during pregnancy.

3. Calcium channel blockers

Common long-acting medicines include amlodipine, felodipine, and extended-release nifedipine. They relax arterial blood vessels, reducing peripheral resistance and lowering blood pressure.

Important adverse effects: ankle swelling, flushing, headache, dizziness, and sometimes palpitations.

Monitoring: blood pressure, dizziness, and peripheral edema.

If ankle swelling develops:

Increasing the calcium channel blocker dose may make the swelling worse. The healthcare professional may consider adjusting the dose or changing the treatment combination.

4. Thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics

Examples include hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, and indapamide. These medicines increase sodium and water excretion and also produce longer-term reduction in vascular resistance.

Important adverse effects: low sodium, low potassium, dehydration, dizziness, and increased uric acid.

Monitoring: sodium, potassium, kidney function, blood pressure, and uric acid when clinically relevant.

When may treatment need to change?

Significant electrolyte abnormalities, dehydration, symptomatic low blood pressure, or clinically important worsening of kidney function may require dose adjustment or a change in treatment.

5. Beta-blockers

Examples include metoprolol, bisoprolol, and carvedilol. Beta-blockers reduce heart rate and cardiac workload and also reduce renin release.

Beta-blockers are particularly useful when hypertension occurs together with conditions such as heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, previous myocardial infarction, angina, or certain cardiac arrhythmias.

Important adverse effects: slow heart rate, low blood pressure, fatigue, dizziness, and possible bronchospasm with susceptible patients.

Monitoring: heart rate, blood pressure, symptoms, and the clinical indication for treatment.

Important:

A beta-blocker that has been used regularly should generally not be stopped suddenly. Abrupt withdrawal can cause clinically important cardiovascular problems in some patients. Dose reduction should be supervised by a healthcare professional.

6. Spironolactone

Spironolactone is an aldosterone antagonist. It promotes sodium excretion while retaining potassium and is particularly useful in selected patients with resistant hypertension.

Important adverse effects: high potassium, changes in kidney function, breast tenderness, and gynecomastia.

Monitoring: serum potassium, kidney function, and blood pressure.

Important:

The risk of high potassium is increased when spironolactone is combined with medicines such as ACE inhibitors or ARBs. Potassium and kidney function should therefore be monitored carefully.

Clinical pharmacy tip:

When blood pressure remains uncontrolled, the solution is not always simply increasing the dose. Medication adherence, measurement technique, lifestyle factors, interacting medicines, secondary causes, adverse effects, and the need for combination therapy should all be considered.

Medicine safety

Taking hypertension medicines correctly is an important part of controlling blood pressure.

  • Take medicines according to the prescribed schedule.
  • Do not stop treatment suddenly without medical advice.
  • Keep a record of blood pressure readings when recommended.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about all medicines, supplements, and herbal products being used.
  • Ask before using over-the-counter medicines, particularly some pain medicines and cold medicines.
  • Report troublesome or unexpected adverse effects.
Drug interactions matter:

Some medicines can increase blood pressure or reduce the effectiveness of antihypertensive treatment. A medication review can help identify these problems.

Common medicines that may affect blood pressure

Some medicines can raise blood pressure or interfere with blood pressure control in certain people.

  • Some non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
  • Some decongestants
  • Certain corticosteroids
  • Some hormonal medicines
  • Certain stimulants

This does not mean that every person must avoid these medicines. The effect depends on the medicine, dose, duration, health conditions, and individual response.

Ask before using:

People with hypertension should check with a healthcare professional before regularly using non-prescription medicines that may affect blood pressure.

Home blood pressure monitoring

Home monitoring can help healthcare professionals understand blood pressure patterns outside the clinic.

For more reliable readings

  • Rest quietly before measuring.
  • Avoid caffeine, exercise, and smoking shortly before measurement when possible.
  • Sit with the back supported.
  • Keep both feet supported.
  • Place the arm at an appropriate level.
  • Record the readings and the time they were taken.

Bring the blood pressure record to healthcare appointments when requested.

When to seek urgent medical care

Very high blood pressure accompanied by symptoms suggesting acute organ damage requires urgent medical assessment.

  • Severe chest pain
  • Severe difficulty breathing
  • Sudden weakness or numbness
  • Difficulty speaking
  • Sudden severe headache with neurological symptoms
  • Sudden major changes in vision
  • Severe confusion
  • Loss of consciousness
Emergency:

Severe symptoms should not be managed by simply taking an extra dose of blood pressure medicine without medical advice. Seek urgent medical care.

Living well with hypertension

Hypertension is usually a long-term condition, but good blood pressure control can substantially reduce health risks.

Successful management involves regular monitoring, appropriate medicine use, healthy lifestyle choices, and regular follow-up with healthcare professionals.

Even when blood pressure becomes controlled, treatment should not be stopped without professional advice.
