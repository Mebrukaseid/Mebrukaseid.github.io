MEDICINES

Diabetes Medicines

Understanding medicines used in diabetes, how they work, important adverse effects, interactions, monitoring, and safe use.

What Are Diabetes Medicines?

Diabetes medicines help control blood glucose and, depending on the medicine and the individual, can also reduce the risk of diabetes-related complications.

The appropriate treatment depends on the type of diabetes, blood-glucose pattern, kidney function, cardiovascular health, weight, pregnancy status, age, other medical conditions, and personal treatment goals.

Important:

Diabetes treatment is individualized. A medicine that is appropriate for one person may not be appropriate for another.

Metformin

Metformin is a commonly used medicine for type 2 diabetes. It mainly reduces glucose production by the liver and improves insulin sensitivity.

Common effects

  • Nausea
  • Diarrhea
  • Abdominal discomfort

Important considerations

Kidney function should be considered when metformin is prescribed. It may need to be temporarily withheld in particular acute clinical situations according to professional guidance.

Long-term use can also be associated with vitamin B12 deficiency in some patients.

SGLT2 Inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors reduce glucose reabsorption in the kidneys, increasing urinary glucose excretion.

Examples

  • Empagliflozin
  • Dapagliflozin
  • Canagliflozin

Some SGLT2 inhibitors have important cardiovascular and kidney benefits in appropriately selected patients, including some people with heart failure or chronic kidney disease.

Important adverse effects

  • Genital fungal infections
  • Increased urination
  • Volume depletion
  • Urinary tract infections in some patients
  • Rare but serious diabetic ketoacidosis

Sulfonylureas

Sulfonylureas stimulate insulin release from pancreatic beta cells.

Examples

  • Glibenclamide (glyburide)
  • Gliclazide
  • Glimepiride

Main concern

Hypoglycemia is an important adverse effect. The risk may be higher in older adults and people with reduced kidney function or irregular food intake.

Weight gain can also occur.

DPP-4 Inhibitors

DPP-4 inhibitors increase the activity of endogenous incretin hormones, helping regulate glucose levels.

Examples

  • Sitagliptin
  • Linagliptin
  • Saxagliptin
  • Alogliptin

They generally have a low risk of hypoglycemia when used alone, although the risk can increase when combined with medicines that cause hypoglycemia.

Some DPP-4 inhibitors require consideration of kidney function when selecting or adjusting treatment.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

GLP-1 receptor agonists enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, reduce glucagon secretion, slow gastric emptying, and can reduce appetite.

Examples

  • Semaglutide
  • Liraglutide
  • Dulaglutide

Important effects

  • Nausea
  • Vomiting
  • Diarrhea or constipation
  • Reduced appetite
  • Weight loss

These medicines may have important cardiovascular benefits in selected patients with type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular risk.

Insulin

Insulin replaces or supplements insulin that the body cannot produce adequately.

Insulin is essential for people with type 1 diabetes and is also used by some people with type 2 diabetes when additional glucose control is needed.

Important risks

  • Hypoglycemia
  • Weight gain
  • Injection-site reactions

Insulin products differ in their onset, duration, formulation, and clinical use.

Hypoglycemia

Hypoglycemia means blood glucose is lower than the desired range and can occur with insulin and some insulin-stimulating medicines.

Possible symptoms

  • Sweating
  • Shaking
  • Hunger
  • Fast heartbeat
  • Dizziness
  • Confusion
  • Weakness
Severe hypoglycemia:

Severe confusion, seizure, loss of consciousness, or inability to safely swallow requires emergency assistance.

Diabetes Medicines & Kidney Function

Kidney function can change the safety or effectiveness of several diabetes medicines.

  • Some medicines require renal dose adjustment.
  • Reduced kidney function can increase the risk of hypoglycemia with some medicines.
  • SGLT2 inhibitor use depends partly on kidney function and the indication.
  • Metformin requires consideration of kidney function.
Important:

Diabetes medicines should not be dose-adjusted based on a general website. Kidney function and the specific medicine must be assessed individually.

Diabetes Medicines & Liver Function

Liver disease can affect glucose metabolism and may influence the choice or monitoring of some diabetes medicines.

Significant liver disease should therefore be discussed with a healthcare professional before starting or changing diabetes treatment.

Pregnancy & Diabetes Medicines

Diabetes during pregnancy requires careful glucose management because both high and low blood glucose can affect the mother and fetus.

Medicine selection during pregnancy should be individualized. Insulin is commonly used when medication is required for diabetes in pregnancy, depending on the clinical situation and local guidance.

Important:

A person who becomes pregnant while taking diabetes medicine should contact a healthcare professional rather than stopping treatment independently.

Important Drug Interactions

Diabetes medicines + other glucose-lowering medicines

Combining several glucose-lowering medicines can increase the risk of hypoglycemia, particularly when insulin or sulfonylureas are involved.

Beta-blockers

Some beta-blockers can mask warning symptoms of hypoglycemia such as rapid heartbeat.

Corticosteroids

Systemic corticosteroids can increase blood glucose and may affect diabetes management.

Other medicines

Many medicines can influence blood glucose. People with diabetes should inform healthcare professionals about all prescription medicines, OTC products, supplements, and herbal preparations.

Diabetes Medicines During Illness

Acute illness can change blood glucose levels and fluid balance. Some medicines may require temporary changes during significant illness, dehydration, surgery, or reduced food intake.

A healthcare professional should provide individualized sick-day instructions, particularly for people using insulin or SGLT2 inhibitors.

Do not make major treatment changes alone.

Ask the diabetes care team what to do when illness, vomiting, dehydration, or poor food intake occurs.

Safe Diabetes Medicine Use at Home

  • Take medicines according to the prescribed plan.
  • Monitor blood glucose when recommended.
  • Know the warning signs of hypoglycemia.
  • Keep a current list of diabetes medicines.
  • Store insulin and other medicines appropriately.
  • Attend regular follow-up appointments.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about changes in other medicines or supplements.

Diabetes & Long-Term Protection

Diabetes management involves more than lowering blood glucose. Cardiovascular risk, kidney health, eye health, nerve health, and foot health are also important.

  • Monitor blood pressure.
  • Manage cholesterol when indicated.
  • Attend recommended kidney screening.
  • Have appropriate eye examinations.
  • Check feet regularly.
  • Maintain appropriate physical activity.
  • Avoid tobacco.

When to Seek Urgent Medical Help

  • Severe or persistent hypoglycemia
  • Loss of consciousness
  • Severe vomiting or dehydration
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Severe weakness or confusion
  • Symptoms suggesting diabetic ketoacidosis
  • Very high blood glucose with serious symptoms

Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Safe diabetes pharmacotherapy requires attention to:

  • Diabetes type
  • Blood glucose and HbA1c
  • Kidney function
  • Liver function when relevant
  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Heart failure
  • Hypoglycemia risk
  • Body weight
  • Pregnancy status
  • Drug interactions
  • Adherence and access to medicines

Educational Information

This page provides general educational information about diabetes medicines and does not replace individualized medical advice.

Diabetes treatment should be selected and adjusted according to the individual's clinical condition, laboratory results, treatment goals, and current clinical guidance.

Know Your Blood Sugar

Monitoring and understanding blood-glucose patterns can help patients and healthcare professionals manage diabetes safely.

Hypoglycemia Awareness

Insulin and some other diabetes medicines can cause low blood glucose. Knowing the warning signs is important for safety.

Protect the Kidneys

Kidney function is an important part of diabetes monitoring and can influence the use of several diabetes medicines.
