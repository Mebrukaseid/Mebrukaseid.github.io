INFECTIOUS RESPIRATORY DISEASE

Influenza

Understanding seasonal influenza, its symptoms, transmission, treatment, prevention, and medicine safety.

What is influenza?

Influenza, commonly called the flu, is a contagious respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses.

Seasonal influenza is mainly caused by influenza A and B viruses. It can range from mild illness to severe disease.

Most people recover without medical treatment, but influenza can cause serious complications, particularly in people at higher risk.

Common symptoms

Influenza symptoms often begin suddenly.

  • Fever or feeling feverish
  • Chills
  • Cough, often dry
  • Sore throat
  • Runny or blocked nose
  • Headache
  • Muscle and body aches
  • Significant tiredness
  • Weakness
  • Vomiting or diarrhea, especially in some children
Important:

Not everyone with influenza has a fever.

How does influenza spread?

Influenza spreads mainly through respiratory particles released when an infected person coughs, sneezes, talks, or breathes.

A person can become infected after close contact with someone who has influenza or after exposure in enclosed spaces where the virus is circulating.

How long can symptoms last?

Many people recover from the main symptoms within about a week, although cough and tiredness can continue for longer.

Who is at higher risk of complications?

Influenza can become more serious in certain groups of people.

  • Pregnant women
  • Children younger than five years, especially very young children
  • Adults aged 65 years and older
  • People with asthma or chronic lung disease
  • People with heart disease
  • People with diabetes
  • People with weakened immune systems
  • People with other significant chronic diseases

Healthcare workers may also have increased exposure to influenza.

Diagnosis

Influenza may be diagnosed based on symptoms and the situation in the community.

Laboratory tests can also detect influenza viruses. Testing may be particularly useful when the result could influence treatment or clinical management.

Because influenza can resemble other respiratory infections, including COVID-19, clinical assessment may be needed when symptoms are significant.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the severity of illness and the person's risk of complications.

Supportive care

People with mild influenza should generally rest, drink adequate fluids, and use appropriate measures to relieve symptoms such as fever and discomfort.

Antiviral medicines

Prescription antiviral medicines can be used to treat influenza.

Antiviral treatment is particularly important for people who are hospitalized, very sick, or at increased risk of serious complications.

Antivirals work best when started as soon as possible, ideally within the first 48 hours after symptoms begin. Treatment may still provide benefit when started later in people with severe illness or higher risk.

Antibiotics

Antibiotics do not treat influenza because influenza is caused by a virus.

An antibiotic may only be considered when a healthcare professional identifies a bacterial infection or bacterial complication requiring treatment.

Important:

Do not self-start antibiotics for flu symptoms. If you are at higher risk or symptoms are severe, contact a healthcare professional promptly to determine whether antiviral treatment is needed.

Home care

Most people with mild influenza can recover at home while monitoring their symptoms.

Rest

Get adequate rest and avoid strenuous activities while feeling unwell.

Drink enough fluids

Drink water and other suitable fluids regularly to help prevent dehydration.

Eat when able

Maintain adequate nutrition as tolerated. Light and nutritious foods may be easier to tolerate when appetite is reduced.

Stay home when sick

Avoid close contact with other people while experiencing influenza symptoms to reduce the chance of spreading infection.

Improve ventilation

Increasing fresh air and ventilation can help reduce exposure to respiratory viruses in indoor spaces.

Remember:

People at higher risk of complications should contact a healthcare professional early rather than relying only on home care.

Medicine safety

  • Antibiotics do not treat influenza.
  • Do not use another person's antiviral medicine.
  • Take prescription antivirals exactly as instructed.
  • Check the active ingredients in combination cold and flu products.
  • Avoid taking multiple products containing the same pain-relieving ingredient.
  • Children require age-appropriate medicines and doses.
  • Tell a healthcare professional about other medicines being used.
  • People who are pregnant or have chronic diseases should seek individualized advice before using medicines.

Prevention

Influenza vaccination

Vaccination is an important way to reduce the risk of influenza and severe complications. Influenza vaccine composition is updated over time because influenza viruses continually change.

Hand hygiene

Wash hands regularly with soap and water or use an appropriate alcohol-based hand rub.

Cover coughs and sneezes

Cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing and dispose of tissues appropriately.

Avoid close contact when sick

Stay home when unwell and avoid close contact with others as much as possible.

Improve indoor ventilation

Open windows or improve airflow when practical, especially in crowded indoor spaces.

Higher-risk groups:

WHO recommends annual influenza vaccination for groups at increased risk, including pregnant women, young children, older adults, people with chronic medical conditions, and healthcare workers.

When to seek medical care

Seek medical assessment when influenza symptoms are severe, worsening, or occur in someone at increased risk of complications.

  • Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
  • Persistent chest pain or pressure
  • Severe weakness or unsteadiness
  • Confusion or difficulty staying awake
  • Severe dehydration or very little urine
  • Seizures
  • Symptoms that improve and then return or worsen
  • Worsening of an existing chronic disease
Emergency:

Severe difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain or pressure, confusion, inability to wake, or other severe symptoms require urgent medical attention.

Educational information

This page provides general health education about seasonal influenza. It does not replace professional examination, diagnosis, or individualized treatment.

If symptoms are severe, persistent, unusual, or worsening, seek assessment from a qualified healthcare professional.

Know the warning signs

Difficulty breathing, chest pain, confusion, severe dehydration, or worsening symptoms require medical attention.

Prevention matters

Influenza vaccination, hand hygiene, ventilation, and reducing contact while sick can help reduce transmission and complications.
