NEUROLOGY & PSYCHIATRY

Neurological &
Psychiatric Medicines

Learn about commonly used medicines for depression, anxiety, psychosis, bipolar disorder, epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, migraine, and other neurological or psychiatric conditions — including important safety considerations and medication-related risks.

What Are Neurological & Psychiatric Medicines?

Neurological and psychiatric medicines are used to manage conditions affecting the brain, nervous system, mood, behavior, sleep, and related functions.

These medicines include antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, antiseizure medicines, medicines used for Parkinson's disease, medicines for anxiety, and selected treatments for migraine and other neurological conditions.

Important:

These medicines often require individualized selection and monitoring. A medicine should not be started, stopped, or changed without appropriate clinical advice.

1. Antidepressants

Antidepressants are commonly used to treat depression and may also be used for several anxiety disorders and other conditions.

Common groups

  • Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)
  • Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)
  • Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)
  • Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)
  • Other antidepressants such as bupropion and mirtazapine

Common examples

  • Sertraline
  • Fluoxetine
  • Escitalopram
  • Paroxetine
  • Venlafaxine
  • Amitriptyline

Important considerations

  • Antidepressants may take several weeks to produce their full therapeutic effect.
  • Common adverse effects may include nausea, headache, sleep changes, or sexual dysfunction.
  • Some antidepressants can interact with other medicines affecting serotonin.
  • Treatment should not be stopped suddenly without an appropriate discontinuation plan.
Interaction warning:

Combining multiple medicines that increase serotonin can increase the risk of serotonin syndrome.

2. Medicines for Anxiety

Anxiety disorders may be treated with psychotherapy, medicines, or a combination of approaches depending on the condition.

Common medicine groups

  • SSRIs
  • SNRIs
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Buspirone
  • Selected medicines used for specific symptoms

Benzodiazepines

Examples include diazepam, lorazepam, and clonazepam. These medicines can reduce anxiety relatively quickly but may cause sedation, tolerance, dependence, and withdrawal problems.

Safety:

Benzodiazepines should be used only as prescribed. Combining them with opioids, alcohol, or other sedating medicines can increase the risk of dangerous respiratory depression and excessive sedation.

3. Antipsychotic Medicines

Antipsychotic medicines are used to treat psychotic symptoms and are also used in conditions such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.

First-generation antipsychotics

  • Haloperidol
  • Chlorpromazine
  • Fluphenazine

Second-generation antipsychotics

  • Risperidone
  • Olanzapine
  • Quetiapine
  • Aripiprazole
  • Clozapine

Important adverse effects

  • Drowsiness and sedation
  • Extrapyramidal symptoms
  • Tardive dyskinesia
  • Weight gain and metabolic abnormalities
  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • QT prolongation with selected medicines
Clozapine:

Clozapine can be highly effective in selected patients but requires appropriate blood monitoring because of the risk of serious blood-related adverse effects.

4. Mood Stabilizers

Mood stabilizers are commonly used in bipolar disorder and other conditions involving significant mood instability.

Common examples

  • Lithium
  • Valproate
  • Carbamazepine
  • Lamotrigine

Lithium monitoring

  • Serum lithium concentration
  • Kidney function
  • Thyroid function
  • Hydration status
  • Important drug interactions
Lithium safety:

Dehydration and some medicines can increase lithium concentrations and contribute to toxicity. Patients taking lithium should follow their monitoring plan carefully.

5. Antiseizure Medicines

Antiseizure medicines are used to prevent or control seizures. Some are also used for neuropathic pain, bipolar disorder, or other conditions.

Common examples

  • Valproate
  • Carbamazepine
  • Phenytoin
  • Levetiracetam
  • Lamotrigine
  • Gabapentin
  • Pregabalin

Important considerations

  • Some medicines require gradual dose changes.
  • Several antiseizure medicines have clinically important drug interactions.
  • Kidney function is important for medicines such as gabapentin and pregabalin.
  • Some medicines require therapeutic drug monitoring in selected patients.

6. Medicines for Parkinson's Disease

Parkinson's disease medicines are used to improve motor symptoms such as slowness, rigidity, and tremor.

Common medicine groups

  • Levodopa/carbidopa
  • Dopamine agonists
  • MAO-B inhibitors
  • COMT inhibitors
  • Amantadine
  • Anticholinergic medicines in selected patients

Important considerations

Treatment is individualized according to symptoms, disease stage, response, adverse effects, age, comorbidities, and other medicines.

Patient safety:

Parkinson's medicines should not be stopped abruptly without medical guidance because sudden interruption of dopaminergic therapy can cause serious complications.

7. Migraine Medicines

Migraine treatment may include medicines for acute attacks and preventive treatment for people with frequent or disabling attacks.

Acute treatment

  • Paracetamol
  • Selected NSAIDs
  • Triptans such as sumatriptan

Preventive treatment

  • Selected beta-blockers
  • Topiramate
  • Valproate in selected situations
  • Selected antidepressants
  • Other preventive therapies according to clinical assessment
Medication-overuse headache:

Frequent use of acute headache medicines can contribute to medication-overuse headache. Recurrent headaches should be assessed rather than repeatedly self-treated.

8. Important Drug Interactions

Neurological and psychiatric medicines can have significant interactions because many affect the central nervous system or are metabolized through hepatic enzymes and transport pathways.

Common interaction patterns

  • Antidepressants + other serotonergic medicines may increase serotonin toxicity risk.
  • Benzodiazepines + opioids or alcohol can increase sedation and respiratory depression.
  • Enzyme-inducing antiseizure medicines can reduce concentrations of several other medicines.
  • Multiple QT-prolonging medicines may increase cardiac rhythm risk.
  • CNS depressants may produce additive sedation.
Clinical pharmacy point:

A complete medication history should include prescription medicines, OTC medicines, herbal products, supplements, and substances such as alcohol.

9. Kidney and Liver Function

Kidney and liver function can significantly influence the selection and dosing of neurological and psychiatric medicines.

  • Some medicines are primarily eliminated by the kidneys and may require dose adjustment.
  • Some medicines undergo extensive hepatic metabolism and may require additional caution in liver disease.
  • Drug levels may be useful for selected medicines with narrow therapeutic ranges.
  • Changes in kidney or liver function may alter exposure and increase adverse-effect risk.

10. Pregnancy and Special Populations

Neurological and psychiatric medicines require particular attention during pregnancy because treatment decisions must consider both maternal health and fetal or neonatal risks.

  • Pregnancy should be discussed before starting, stopping, or changing long-term therapy.
  • Some antiseizure medicines have important pregnancy-related safety considerations.
  • Older adults may be more sensitive to sedation, orthostatic hypotension, and anticholinergic effects.
  • Children and adolescents may require age- and weight-appropriate treatment.
Important:

A person who becomes pregnant while taking a neurological or psychiatric medicine should seek professional advice rather than stopping treatment suddenly.

11. Important Adverse Effects

Adverse effects vary substantially between medicines. Some important patterns include:

  • Sedation and impaired alertness
  • Dizziness and falls
  • Extrapyramidal symptoms
  • Weight gain and metabolic abnormalities
  • Sexual dysfunction
  • Gastrointestinal effects
  • Cognitive effects
  • Serious skin reactions with selected medicines
  • Blood abnormalities with selected medicines

Any new, severe, or rapidly worsening adverse effect should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

12. Why Some Medicines Should Not Be Stopped Suddenly

Some neurological and psychiatric medicines require gradual dose reduction when discontinuation is appropriate.

Abrupt discontinuation may result in withdrawal symptoms, rebound symptoms, recurrence of the underlying condition, or other complications depending on the medicine.

  • Some antidepressants
  • Benzodiazepines
  • Antiseizure medicines
  • Antipsychotic medicines
  • Dopaminergic medicines used in Parkinson's disease
Patient safety:

Never change or stop long-term neurological or psychiatric treatment without discussing the plan with the healthcare professional responsible for the treatment.

13. Monitoring and Follow-Up

Monitoring depends on the medicine and the condition being treated.

  • Treatment response
  • Adverse effects
  • Medication adherence
  • Kidney function
  • Liver function
  • Weight and metabolic parameters when appropriate
  • Blood counts for selected medicines
  • Therapeutic drug concentrations for selected medicines
  • Drug interactions

14. Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Clinical pharmacy contributes to safer and more effective neurological and psychiatric medicine use.

  • Reviewing the indication for each medicine
  • Checking dose and administration schedule
  • Identifying clinically important interactions
  • Reviewing kidney and liver function
  • Monitoring treatment response
  • Detecting adverse drug reactions
  • Identifying unnecessary duplication
  • Supporting medication adherence
  • Educating patients and caregivers
  • Supporting safe medication discontinuation when clinically appropriate
Patient-centered care:

Effective treatment is not only about selecting a medicine. It also involves monitoring response, reducing medication-related risks, and helping patients understand their treatment.

15. Patient Education

  • Take medicines exactly as prescribed.
  • Do not share prescription medicines with others.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment suddenly unless specifically instructed.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about all medicines, supplements, and herbal products being used.
  • Ask about drowsiness and whether driving or operating machinery is safe.
  • Keep scheduled laboratory and clinical follow-up appointments when monitoring is required.
  • Report serious or unexpected adverse effects.

16. When to Seek Urgent Medical Help

Some symptoms occurring during neurological or psychiatric treatment require urgent assessment.

  • Severe difficulty breathing or loss of consciousness
  • Severe confusion or sudden major change in behavior
  • Severe allergic reaction
  • Severe muscle rigidity with fever or altered consciousness
  • Severe uncontrolled movements
  • Seizure lasting unusually long or repeated seizures without recovery
  • Severe suicidal thoughts or immediate danger to self or others
Emergency:

Severe or rapidly worsening symptoms require immediate medical attention rather than adjustment of medicines without professional guidance.

Medicine Safety

Neurological and psychiatric medicines can affect alertness, behavior, movement, mood, and other important functions. Appropriate monitoring is essential.
