RESPIRATORY DISEASE

Asthma

Understanding asthma, its symptoms, triggers, treatment options, inhalers, stepwise management, acute attacks, and medicine safety.

What is asthma?

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease in which the airways become inflamed and narrowed, making it more difficult for air to move in and out of the lungs.

Asthma symptoms can vary over time and may be triggered or worsened by factors such as viral respiratory infections, smoke, allergens, exercise, cold air, and occupational exposures.

With appropriate treatment, many people with asthma can control their symptoms and maintain normal daily activities. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

Important:

Asthma is not simply a condition that requires an occasional reliever inhaler. Long-term treatment should address airway inflammation and reduce the risk of exacerbations.

Common symptoms

  • Wheezing
  • Shortness of breath
  • Chest tightness
  • Cough, especially at night or early morning
  • Symptoms that worsen with exercise
  • Symptoms that vary over time

Symptoms can be mild at one time and severe at another. Some people experience symptoms only occasionally, while others have frequent symptoms.

Common asthma triggers

  • Tobacco smoke
  • Air pollution
  • Dust and dust mites
  • Mold
  • Pollen
  • Animal allergens
  • Viral respiratory infections
  • Cold air
  • Exercise
  • Strong fragrances and chemical fumes
  • Occupational dusts and chemicals

Triggers differ between individuals. Identifying personal triggers can help reduce symptoms.

Diagnosis

Asthma diagnosis is based on the pattern of respiratory symptoms together with evidence of variable expiratory airflow limitation.

Assessment may include

  • Medical history
  • Physical examination
  • Spirometry
  • Bronchodilator reversibility testing
  • Peak expiratory flow monitoring in selected situations
  • Assessment of allergies or other conditions when appropriate
Important:

Cough or wheezing alone does not automatically mean asthma. Other respiratory and cardiac conditions can produce similar symptoms.

Different asthma patterns

Asthma can occur in different clinical patterns. Identifying associated characteristics can help guide treatment.

Allergic asthma

Symptoms are associated with allergic sensitization or exposure to allergens. It may occur together with conditions such as allergic rhinitis or eczema.

Exercise-related symptoms

Exercise can trigger airway narrowing in some people with asthma. Good overall asthma control remains important.

Occupational asthma

Asthma may be caused or worsened by workplace exposures such as dusts, chemicals, fumes, or other respiratory irritants.

Severe asthma

Some people continue to have poor asthma control despite optimized treatment, correct inhaler technique, good adherence, and management of contributing factors. These patients may require specialist assessment.

Main asthma medicine groups

Asthma medicines can broadly be divided into medicines that control airway inflammation and medicines that provide rapid relief of bronchoconstriction.

1. Inhaled corticosteroids — ICS

Examples include:

  • Budesonide
  • Beclometasone
  • Fluticasone

ICS medicines reduce airway inflammation and are the foundation of long-term asthma control. They reduce symptoms and the risk of severe exacerbations.

2. Short-acting beta-2 agonists — SABA

Example: Salbutamol

SABA medicines rapidly relax airway smooth muscle and can relieve bronchoconstriction.

Important:

Frequent reliance on a SABA without adequate anti-inflammatory treatment is associated with increased risk of asthma exacerbations. Current GINA guidance favors ICS-containing treatment rather than SABA-only treatment. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

3. ICS-formoterol

Formoterol is a long-acting beta-2 agonist with a rapid onset of action. When combined with an inhaled corticosteroid such as budesonide, the inhaler can provide both anti-inflammatory treatment and bronchodilation.

Depending on the patient's treatment plan, ICS-formoterol may be used as both maintenance and reliever therapy, commonly called MART.

4. Long-acting beta-2 agonists — LABA

LABAs such as formoterol and salmeterol provide prolonged bronchodilation.

Safety:

LABAs should not be used alone for asthma. They should be used together with an inhaled corticosteroid.

5. Leukotriene receptor antagonists

An example is montelukast . It may be useful in selected patients, particularly when allergic rhinitis or exercise-related symptoms are relevant.

Neuropsychiatric adverse effects should be discussed and monitored when montelukast is used.

6. Long-acting muscarinic antagonists — LAMA

Tiotropium is an example. LAMA therapy can be considered as an additional treatment in selected patients whose asthma remains uncontrolled.

Controller vs reliever medicines

Controller treatment

Controller treatment is taken to reduce airway inflammation and prevent symptoms and exacerbations.

Examples include:

  • Inhaled corticosteroids
  • ICS-LABA combinations
  • ICS-formoterol MART regimens
  • Other add-on therapies in selected patients

Reliever treatment

Reliever treatment is used when symptoms occur and is intended to provide rapid improvement in airflow.

Depending on the treatment pathway, this may include ICS-formoterol or another appropriate bronchodilator strategy.

Remember:

A reliever can make breathing feel better, but asthma also involves airway inflammation. Good long-term management therefore requires appropriate ICS-containing treatment.

Stepwise asthma treatment

Asthma treatment is adjusted according to symptom control, exacerbation risk, inhaler technique, adherence, comorbidities, and individual response.

Step 1–2: Mild asthma

Current GINA treatment pathways prioritize an ICS-containing reliever strategy for adults and adolescents rather than treatment with a bronchodilator alone.

Step 3: More persistent symptoms

Patients may require regular anti-inflammatory treatment and, depending on the treatment track, maintenance-and-reliever therapy with ICS-formoterol.

Step 4: More difficult-to-control asthma

Treatment intensity may be increased after checking adherence, inhaler technique, diagnosis, trigger exposure, and comorbidities.

Step 5: Severe or difficult-to-treat asthma

Specialist assessment may be required. Additional therapies, including biologic medicines, may be considered for selected patients with severe asthma.

Very important:

Before stepping up treatment, healthcare professionals should check whether poor control is caused by incorrect inhaler technique, poor adherence, continued trigger exposure, smoking, an incorrect diagnosis, or untreated comorbidities.

When should treatment be stepped up?

Treatment may need to be increased when asthma remains poorly controlled despite appropriate treatment.

Before increasing medicines, assess:

  • Inhaler technique
  • Medication adherence
  • Exposure to triggers
  • Smoking or vaping exposure
  • Correctness of the asthma diagnosis
  • Allergic rhinitis or other comorbidities
  • Frequency of exacerbations

If these factors are addressed and control remains inadequate, treatment intensity may be increased according to the appropriate treatment pathway.

When can asthma treatment be stepped down?

If asthma has remained well controlled for a sustained period, treatment may sometimes be stepped down carefully to the lowest treatment intensity that maintains good control.

Important:

Inhaled corticosteroids should not simply be stopped because symptoms have improved. Step-down decisions should be individualized and supervised.

Correct inhaler technique

A medicine can be effective but provide little benefit if the inhaler is used incorrectly.

General principles

  • Use the inhaler exactly as instructed
  • Check the inhaler type and technique regularly
  • Breathe out before inhaling the medicine when appropriate for the device
  • Inhale using the technique required for the specific inhaler
  • Hold the breath when appropriate
  • Use a spacer with a suitable pressurized metered-dose inhaler when recommended
  • Clean and maintain the device according to instructions

Spacer devices can help aerosol medicine reach the lungs more effectively and can make inhaler use easier. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

Inhaled corticosteroid safety

ICS medicines are generally well tolerated when used appropriately.

Common local effects

  • Oral thrush
  • Hoarseness
  • Throat irritation

Rinsing the mouth after using an inhaled corticosteroid can help reduce local adverse effects.

Higher doses used for prolonged periods can increase the risk of systemic corticosteroid effects, so treatment should be kept at the lowest effective intensity.

Asthma attack or exacerbation

An asthma exacerbation is a worsening of symptoms and airflow limitation that requires additional treatment.

Warning symptoms

  • Increasing shortness of breath
  • Increasing wheezing
  • Increasing cough
  • Difficulty speaking because of breathlessness
  • Increasing use of reliever medication
  • Reduced peak expiratory flow when monitored

Emergency features

  • Severe difficulty breathing
  • Unable to speak normally because of breathlessness
  • Drowsiness or confusion
  • Blue or grey lips or skin
  • Very low oxygen saturation when measured
  • A very quiet or silent chest despite severe breathing difficulty
Emergency:

Severe or life-threatening asthma requires urgent medical treatment. Do not delay emergency care while attempting to manage a severe attack at home.

Treatment of an acute asthma attack

Acute asthma treatment depends on severity.

Bronchodilator treatment

Rapid-acting bronchodilator treatment such as salbutamol may be used during an acute attack. In some situations ipratropium is added.

Corticosteroids

Systemic corticosteroids may be required for moderate or severe exacerbations according to clinical assessment.

Oxygen

Oxygen may be given when clinically indicated during a severe exacerbation.

GINA 2026 recommends urgent transfer and immediate treatment for severe or life-threatening asthma, including appropriate bronchodilator therapy, oxygen, and systemic corticosteroids. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Severe asthma and biologic medicines

Some people continue to have uncontrolled asthma despite optimized inhaled treatment and good adherence.

Specialist assessment may identify a severe asthma phenotype for which biologic treatment may be appropriate.

Depending on the patient's characteristics, specialist treatment may target pathways involving IgE or type 2 inflammation.

Examples of biologic medicines include agents such as omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab, dupilumab, and tezepelumab.

Important:

Biologic therapy is not a routine treatment for ordinary mild asthma. Selection requires specialist assessment and consideration of phenotype, biomarkers, exacerbation history, and previous treatment.

Asthma and other conditions

Other medical conditions can worsen asthma symptoms or make asthma more difficult to control.

  • Allergic rhinitis
  • Chronic sinus disease
  • Gastroesophageal reflux
  • Obesity
  • Anxiety or depression
  • Obstructive sleep apnea
  • Smoking-related respiratory disease

Appropriate assessment and treatment of clinically relevant comorbidities can improve asthma control.

Medicines that require caution

Some medicines can worsen asthma or trigger bronchospasm in susceptible people.

Beta-blockers

Some beta-blockers can cause bronchospasm. Their use should be carefully considered in people with asthma, especially when alternatives are available.

Aspirin and NSAIDs

Some people with asthma develop respiratory symptoms after taking aspirin or other NSAIDs. This is particularly important in people with a history of NSAID-sensitive asthma.

Herbal and OTC medicines

Herbal products and over-the-counter medicines can have adverse effects or interactions. People with asthma should tell their healthcare professional about all products they use.

Living well with asthma

  • Use controller treatment as prescribed
  • Learn correct inhaler technique
  • Identify personal triggers
  • Avoid tobacco smoke
  • Follow an individualized asthma action plan
  • Keep recommended medicines available
  • Attend regular follow-up
  • Ask for an inhaler technique review

When to seek urgent medical care

  • Severe difficulty breathing
  • Symptoms rapidly worsening
  • Unable to speak comfortably because of breathlessness
  • Blue or grey lips or skin
  • Confusion or unusual drowsiness
  • Reliever treatment is not providing adequate improvement
  • Severe chest tightness or exhaustion

Educational information

This page provides general educational information about asthma. It does not replace diagnosis, an individualized asthma action plan, or treatment from a qualified healthcare professional.

Asthma treatment should be reviewed regularly according to symptom control, exacerbations, inhaler technique, adherence, risk factors, comorbidities, and treatment response.

Control the inflammation

Asthma is an inflammatory airway disease. Appropriate ICS-containing treatment helps reduce the risk of severe attacks.

Check inhaler technique

Incorrect inhaler technique can make effective medicines appear ineffective.

Stay active

With appropriate asthma control, many people can remain physically active and participate in normal daily activities.
