ANTICOAGULATION

Anticoagulant Medicines

Learn about commonly used anticoagulants, their uses, bleeding risks, important interactions, monitoring, renal considerations, and safe medicine use.

What Are Anticoagulants?

Anticoagulants are medicines that reduce the formation or growth of harmful blood clots. They are sometimes called "blood thinners," although they do not actually make the blood thinner.

They are used in conditions where preventing harmful clot formation is important, including some cases of atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and pulmonary embolism (PE). :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

Important:

Anticoagulants are different from antiplatelet medicines such as aspirin and clopidogrel. Both can reduce clot-related events, but they work through different mechanisms.

Common Anticoagulant Medicines

Warfarin

Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist. It is an oral anticoagulant used for several indications, including prevention and treatment of venous thromboembolism and prevention of thromboembolic events in selected patients with atrial fibrillation or mechanical heart valves.

Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

Common DOACs include:

  • Apixaban
  • Rivaroxaban
  • Dabigatran
  • Edoxaban

DOAC selection and dosing depend on the indication, kidney function, age, weight, interacting medicines, and other patient characteristics. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Heparins

Injectable anticoagulants include unfractionated heparin (UFH) and low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs), such as enoxaparin.

These medicines are commonly encountered in hospital practice for treatment or prevention of thromboembolic disease.

Why Are Anticoagulants Used?

  • Atrial fibrillation in patients who require stroke prevention
  • Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)
  • Pulmonary embolism (PE)
  • Prevention of venous thromboembolism in selected high-risk patients
  • Prevention of thromboembolism in selected patients with mechanical heart valves
  • Other specific thromboembolic conditions according to clinical assessment

Warfarin

Warfarin reduces the activity of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

INR Monitoring

Warfarin requires regular blood testing using the international normalized ratio (INR). The dose is adjusted according to the patient's clinical indication and INR results. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

Important Food Consideration

Vitamin-K-containing foods can affect warfarin treatment. Patients should generally maintain consistency in their dietary vitamin K intake rather than making sudden major changes. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

Important Interactions

  • NSAIDs may increase bleeding risk.
  • Aspirin may increase bleeding risk unless specifically indicated.
  • Many antibiotics can affect anticoagulation control.
  • Some antifungals and other medicines can alter warfarin exposure.
  • Some herbal products can interfere with anticoagulation.

Because warfarin has many clinically important interactions, a new medicine should be reviewed before it is started. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

Direct Oral Anticoagulants

Apixaban

Apixaban is a direct factor Xa inhibitor. It is used for several thromboembolic indications, including stroke prevention in selected patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation and treatment or prevention of DVT and PE.

Rivaroxaban

Rivaroxaban is also a direct factor Xa inhibitor used for several thromboembolic indications.

Edoxaban

Edoxaban is a direct factor Xa inhibitor. Appropriate dosing depends on the indication and patient-specific factors including kidney function.

Dabigatran

Dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor. Kidney function is particularly important because renal elimination contributes substantially to its clearance.

DOAC dosing should be based on the specific medicine, indication, and approved product information. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

Kidney Function and Anticoagulants

Kidney function is an important consideration for several anticoagulants because impaired renal clearance can increase drug exposure and bleeding risk.

DOAC Renal Assessment

For DOAC dosing, the Specialist Pharmacy Service recommends calculating creatinine clearance using the Cockcroft–Gault method. Estimated GFR should not automatically be substituted when dosing these medicines. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

Dabigatran

Dabigatran is particularly dependent on renal elimination, so reduced creatinine clearance can substantially affect exposure.

Apixaban

Apixaban dosing may need adjustment depending on indication, renal function, age, body weight, and serum creatinine.

Edoxaban

Edoxaban requires indication-specific assessment of creatinine clearance. Dose reduction may be required at lower renal function.

Rivaroxaban

Rivaroxaban dosing also depends on the clinical indication and renal function.

Clinical pharmacy point:

Never change a DOAC dose based only on a general "kidney disease" label. The exact anticoagulant, indication, creatinine clearance, age, weight, and other factors must be considered.

Major Adverse Effect: Bleeding

The most important adverse effect associated with anticoagulants is excessive bleeding.

Possible Warning Signs

  • Unusual or severe bruising
  • Prolonged nosebleeds
  • Bleeding gums
  • Blood in urine
  • Black or bloody stool
  • Vomiting blood
  • Coughing up blood
  • Severe or persistent bleeding

These symptoms require appropriate medical assessment. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

Important Drug Interactions

Anticoagulants + NSAIDs

NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac can increase bleeding risk when combined with anticoagulants.

Anticoagulants + Antiplatelets

Combining an anticoagulant with aspirin, clopidogrel, or another antiplatelet can increase bleeding risk. Such combinations may nevertheless be indicated in specific clinical situations and should be medically supervised.

Anticoagulants + Strong Enzyme Modifiers

Some medicines affect CYP enzymes or drug transporters and can alter the exposure of certain DOACs.

Anticoagulants + Herbal Products

Herbal medicines and supplements can also interact with anticoagulants. St John's wort, for example, has important interaction potential with several medicines. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

Food, Alcohol, and Anticoagulants

Warfarin and Vitamin K

Patients taking warfarin should avoid sudden major changes in their usual intake of vitamin-K-rich foods.

DOACs and Food

Food instructions differ between DOACs and indications. For example, some rivaroxaban doses must be taken with food, while apixaban can generally be taken with or without food. Always follow the specific product instructions.

Alcohol

Excessive alcohol intake can increase bleeding risk and may affect safe anticoagulant use. Alcohol intake should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

Monitoring

Warfarin

  • INR
  • Full blood count
  • Liver function
  • Kidney function when clinically relevant
  • Bleeding assessment
  • Medication interaction review

DOACs

DOACs do not require routine INR monitoring, but this does not mean that monitoring is unnecessary.

Baseline and ongoing assessment can include full blood count, liver function, urea and electrolytes, serum creatinine/creatinine clearance, adherence, bleeding, and interacting medicines. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

More frequent assessment may be needed in older or frail patients, patients with renal impairment, or those with relevant comorbidities or intercurrent illness. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

Heparins

Unfractionated Heparin

Unfractionated heparin is an injectable anticoagulant commonly used in hospital settings. Its short duration of action and reversibility can be useful in selected situations.

Low-Molecular-Weight Heparins

Examples include enoxaparin and dalteparin. They are used for prevention and treatment of thromboembolic conditions.

Important Safety Issues

  • Bleeding
  • Injection-site reactions
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Renal function considerations

Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Anticoagulant choice during pregnancy requires specialist assessment because the safety of anticoagulants differs between medicines.

Many oral anticoagulants are not routinely used during pregnancy. Anyone who becomes pregnant while taking an anticoagulant should contact their healthcare professional promptly rather than stopping treatment independently. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

Important:

Anticoagulant treatment should not be stopped suddenly without medical advice because the risk of thrombosis may return.

Anticoagulants and Surgery

Patients taking anticoagulants should inform their healthcare team before surgery, dental procedures, endoscopy, or other invasive procedures.

Depending on the medicine and procedure, treatment may need temporary interruption or another carefully planned strategy.

Patients should not independently stop their anticoagulant before a procedure. :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}

Missed Doses

Instructions for a missed anticoagulant dose depend on the specific medicine.

Warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, and edoxaban do not all have identical missed-dose instructions.

Never double the dose automatically.

Check the medicine-specific patient information or contact a pharmacist or healthcare professional for advice.

Patient Counseling

  • Take the medicine exactly as prescribed.
  • Do not stop anticoagulation without medical advice.
  • Tell every healthcare professional that you take an anticoagulant.
  • Ask before taking OTC pain medicines.
  • Report unusual bleeding.
  • Tell healthcare professionals about herbal medicines and supplements.
  • Keep required monitoring appointments.
  • Carry an anticoagulant alert card or other treatment information when provided.

Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Anticoagulants are high-risk medicines, so medication review should consider both the benefit of preventing thrombosis and the risk of bleeding.

  • Confirm the indication.
  • Confirm the correct medicine and dose.
  • Assess kidney function.
  • Assess liver function when relevant.
  • Review age and body weight.
  • Check interacting medicines.
  • Assess bleeding risk.
  • Assess adherence.
  • Review laboratory monitoring.
  • Provide patient education.

When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

  • Severe or uncontrolled bleeding
  • Vomiting blood
  • Coughing up blood
  • Black or bloody stool
  • Blood in urine with significant bleeding
  • Severe headache or neurological symptoms
  • Significant head injury while taking an anticoagulant
  • Symptoms suggesting stroke or pulmonary embolism
Emergency:

Serious bleeding or symptoms of a major blood clot require urgent medical assessment.

Educational Note

This page provides general educational information about anticoagulant medicines. It does not replace an individualized prescription, anticoagulation service, pharmacist review, or medical assessment.

Bleeding Risk

Anticoagulants can cause serious bleeding. Report unusual or severe bleeding promptly.

Monitoring Matters

Warfarin requires INR monitoring. DOACs do not require routine INR monitoring, but kidney, liver, blood-count, adherence, and interaction reviews remain important.

Clinical Pharmacy

Anticoagulant review includes indication, dose, kidney function, interactions, bleeding risk, and patient education.
