MEDICINE INFORMATION

Asthma
Medicines

Medicines used to control asthma symptoms, reduce airway inflammation, prevent exacerbations, and provide relief when symptoms occur.

What are asthma medicines?

Asthma medicines are used to control airway inflammation, relieve bronchoconstriction, prevent asthma attacks, and manage worsening symptoms.

Asthma treatment is individualized according to symptom control, risk of exacerbations, age, response to treatment, inhaler technique, adherence, and other clinical factors.

Important:

Current GINA guidance recommends that asthma treatment include an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)-containing approach rather than relying on a short-acting bronchodilator alone.

Controller and Reliever Medicines

Controller medicines

Controller medicines are taken regularly or as part of an appropriate treatment strategy to reduce airway inflammation and prevent symptoms and exacerbations.

Reliever medicines

Reliever medicines are used when asthma symptoms occur. Current GINA guidance favors an anti-inflammatory reliever containing an ICS with a rapid bronchodilator when appropriate.

Key point:

Frequent need for a reliever can indicate poor asthma control and should prompt assessment of treatment, adherence, inhaler technique, and possible triggers.

1. Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS)

Inhaled corticosteroids reduce inflammation in the airways and are a fundamental component of asthma treatment.

Common examples

  • Budesonide
  • Beclometasone
  • Fluticasone
  • Mometasone

Common adverse effects

  • Oral thrush
  • Hoarseness or dysphonia
  • Throat irritation

Rinsing the mouth after using an ICS-containing inhaler and using the inhaler correctly can reduce local adverse effects.

2. ICS-Formoterol

Formoterol is a rapid-onset long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA) that can be combined with an inhaled corticosteroid.

In appropriate patients, an ICS-formoterol inhaler may be used as an anti-inflammatory reliever and, in maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART), for both maintenance and symptom relief.

Common combination

  • Budesonide-formoterol
Important:

The specific inhaler strength, dose, and treatment regimen should follow the prescribed asthma plan.

3. Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (SABA)

SABAs rapidly relax airway smooth muscle and can provide quick relief of bronchoconstriction.

Common example

  • Salbutamol (albuterol)

Possible adverse effects

  • Tremor
  • Palpitations
  • Fast heart rate
  • Headache
  • Low potassium with excessive use
SABA overuse:

Frequent use of SABA can be a warning sign of inadequate asthma control and is associated with increased risk of exacerbations.

4. Long-Acting Beta-2 Agonists (LABA)

LABAs provide prolonged bronchodilation and are used with inhaled corticosteroids in asthma.

Common examples

  • Formoterol
  • Salmeterol
Important safety point:

LABAs should not be used alone for asthma treatment. In asthma, they are used together with an inhaled corticosteroid.

5. Long-Acting Muscarinic Antagonists (LAMA)

LAMAs provide prolonged bronchodilation and may be added to ICS-LABA treatment in selected patients whose asthma remains uncontrolled.

Common example

  • Tiotropium

LAMA therapy is generally an add-on treatment rather than the basic treatment for asthma.

6. Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists

Leukotriene receptor antagonists reduce leukotriene activity and can be useful in selected patients.

Common example

  • Montelukast
Safety:

Montelukast has important neuropsychiatric warnings. Patients and caregivers should discuss concerning mood or behavioral changes with a healthcare professional.

7. Systemic Corticosteroids

Oral or intravenous corticosteroids may be used during moderate or severe asthma exacerbations when clinically indicated.

Common examples

  • Prednisolone
  • Prednisone
  • Methylprednisolone

Long-term oral corticosteroid use should generally be avoided when possible because of significant systemic adverse effects. GINA emphasizes optimizing inhaled therapy and considering appropriate add-on treatment to reduce the need for repeated systemic corticosteroid exposure.

8. Biologic Medicines

Biologic medicines are specialized treatments for selected patients with severe asthma that remains uncontrolled despite optimized standard treatment.

Examples

  • Omalizumab
  • Mepolizumab
  • Benralizumab
  • Dupilumab
  • Tezepelumab

Selection depends on the patient's asthma phenotype, biomarkers, exacerbation history, comorbidities, eligibility criteria, and response to previous treatment.

Inhaler Technique

Correct inhaler technique is essential because poor technique can result in inadequate drug delivery and poor asthma control.

  • Use the inhaler exactly as instructed.
  • Learn the correct technique for the specific inhaler.
  • Use a spacer with an appropriate pressurized metered-dose inhaler when recommended.
  • Ask a healthcare professional to check inhaler technique.
  • Do not assume that every inhaler is used in the same way.

Important Medicine Considerations

Beta-blockers

Some beta-blockers can worsen bronchoconstriction in susceptible patients. The need for a beta-blocker should therefore be assessed carefully in people with asthma.

Aspirin and NSAIDs

Aspirin and other NSAIDs can trigger respiratory symptoms in susceptible people, particularly those with aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease.

Other medicines

A complete medication history is important because other medicines may affect asthma control or interact with asthma treatment.

Pregnancy

Maintaining good asthma control during pregnancy is important for both the pregnant person and the fetus.

Asthma medicines should not be stopped automatically because of pregnancy. Treatment decisions should be individualized with appropriate healthcare advice.

Children

Asthma treatment in children depends on age, symptoms, severity, exacerbation risk, inhaler device, and the child's ability to use the device correctly.

Parents and caregivers should receive clear instructions about inhaler technique and the child's asthma action plan.

When Asthma Needs Urgent Help

Severe or rapidly worsening asthma symptoms can be a medical emergency.

  • Severe difficulty breathing
  • Difficulty speaking because of breathlessness
  • Severe chest tightness or wheezing
  • Very rapid worsening despite reliever treatment
  • Confusion, drowsiness, or exhaustion
  • Blue or gray discoloration of the lips or skin
Emergency:

Severe breathing difficulty requires urgent medical assessment and should not be managed only by repeatedly using an inhaler at home.

Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Clinical pharmacy care in asthma includes checking inhaler technique, adherence, medicine selection, treatment response, adverse effects, interactions, trigger exposure, and the patient's asthma action plan.

Pharmacists can also help patients understand the difference between maintenance treatment and reliever treatment and recognize signs of poor asthma control.

Patient Education

  • Use asthma medicines exactly as prescribed.
  • Do not stop controller treatment without medical advice.
  • Learn and regularly review inhaler technique.
  • Keep an up-to-date asthma action plan.
  • Know which inhaler is used for maintenance and which is used for relief.
  • Seek medical help when symptoms become severe or rapidly worsen.
Remember:

Asthma is not only a problem of airway tightening. Airway inflammation is also central to the disease, which is why ICS-containing treatment is important.

Asthma Safety

Frequent reliever use, worsening symptoms, or severe breathing difficulty should prompt medical assessment.

Healthy Breathing

Good asthma control depends on appropriate medicines, correct inhaler technique, adherence, and management of triggers.
