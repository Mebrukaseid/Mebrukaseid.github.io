CARDIOVASCULAR HEALTH

Heart Failure

Understanding the different types of heart failure, symptoms, causes, diagnosis, treatment options, medicines, monitoring, and everyday care.

What is heart failure?

Heart failure is a condition in which the heart cannot pump or fill with blood well enough to meet the body's needs.

Heart failure does not mean that the heart has stopped working. It means that the heart's pumping or filling ability is impaired.

Heart failure can develop suddenly or gradually and can result from many different conditions, including coronary artery disease, previous heart attack, hypertension, heart valve disease, cardiomyopathy, abnormal heart rhythms, and other diseases.

Important:

Heart failure is a serious medical condition, but appropriate treatment can reduce symptoms, prevent hospitalizations, and improve quality of life. Treatment depends strongly on the type and severity of heart failure.

Different types of heart failure

Heart failure can be classified according to ejection fraction (EF), the percentage of blood pumped out of the left ventricle with each heartbeat.

1. Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction — HFrEF

HFrEF is generally defined as heart failure with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40% or less.

The heart's pumping function is reduced. This group has the strongest evidence for multiple disease-modifying medicines.

2. Heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction — HFmrEF

HFmrEF generally refers to an ejection fraction of approximately 41–49% in a patient with clinical evidence of heart failure.

Treatment overlaps with HFrEF in some areas, but the strength of evidence for individual medicines is not identical.

3. Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction — HFpEF

HFpEF generally refers to an ejection fraction of 50% or greater together with evidence supporting the diagnosis of heart failure.

The heart may contract relatively normally but may be stiff and unable to relax and fill normally.

4. Heart failure with improved ejection fraction — HFimpEF

HFimpEF describes a person who previously had reduced ejection fraction and later has an improvement in ejection fraction.

Important:

Improvement in ejection fraction does not necessarily mean that the underlying heart failure has permanently disappeared. Guideline-directed treatment is generally continued unless a healthcare professional determines otherwise.

Heart failure stages

Heart failure can also be described using stages that reflect progression from risk to advanced disease.

Stage A — At risk

The person is at risk of developing heart failure but does not currently have structural heart disease or symptoms of heart failure.

Examples of risk factors include hypertension, diabetes, obesity, cardiovascular disease, exposure to cardiotoxic treatments, and some genetic conditions.

Stage B — Pre-heart failure

There is evidence of structural heart disease, abnormal cardiac function, or other objective evidence suggesting pre-heart failure, but typical symptoms have not developed.

Stage C — Symptomatic heart failure

Structural heart disease is present together with current or previous symptoms or signs of heart failure.

Stage D — Advanced heart failure

Heart failure has become difficult to manage despite appropriate treatment and may interfere substantially with daily activities or result in repeated hospitalizations.

Clinical concept:

EF classification and clinical stage answer different questions. A person can have HFrEF and be Stage C, for example. Both classifications help guide management.

Causes of heart failure

Heart failure can result from many conditions that damage or overload the heart.

  • Coronary artery disease
  • Previous myocardial infarction
  • Long-standing hypertension
  • Heart valve disease
  • Cardiomyopathy
  • Abnormal heart rhythms
  • Congenital heart disease
  • Diabetes and metabolic disease
  • Some infections or inflammatory conditions
  • Certain cardiotoxic medicines or treatments
  • Some genetic heart conditions

Common symptoms and signs

  • Shortness of breath
  • Difficulty breathing when lying flat
  • Waking at night because of breathlessness
  • Swelling of the legs, ankles, or abdomen
  • Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance
  • Persistent cough or wheezing
  • Rapid or irregular heartbeat
  • Sudden or unexplained weight gain from fluid retention
  • Reduced appetite or feeling full quickly
Worsening heart failure:

Increasing breathlessness, rapidly increasing swelling, or rapid weight gain can indicate fluid accumulation and should be discussed promptly with a healthcare professional.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines symptoms, physical examination, medical history, laboratory tests, imaging, and other cardiac investigations.

Important investigations

  • Echocardiography
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG)
  • Chest imaging when appropriate
  • BNP or NT-proBNP when clinically appropriate
  • Kidney function and electrolytes
  • Complete blood count when indicated
  • Liver function assessment when appropriate
  • Cardiac biomarkers in selected situations

Echocardiography is particularly important because it can evaluate ejection fraction, chamber size, valve function, and other structural abnormalities.

Treatment of HFrEF

HFrEF has several evidence-based disease-modifying medicine groups. Current guideline-directed therapy centers on four major classes.

The four pillars of HFrEF treatment

  • ARNI, or an ACE inhibitor/ARB when appropriate
  • Evidence-based beta-blocker
  • Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA)
  • SGLT-2 inhibitor

Diuretics are also used when fluid retention or congestion is present.

Clinical pharmacy point:

HFrEF treatment is usually a combination strategy rather than relying on one medicine. Current expert guidance supports introducing the core therapies early and gradually titrating them to doses tolerated by the patient.

1. ARNI / ACE inhibitor / ARB

ARNI

The main ARNI used in HFrEF is sacubitril/valsartan.

Sacubitril inhibits neprilysin while valsartan blocks the angiotensin II receptor. The combination reduces harmful neurohormonal effects and supports favorable cardiovascular effects.

ACE inhibitors

Examples include enalapril, lisinopril, and ramipril.

ARBs

Examples include losartan, valsartan, and candesartan.

Important monitoring

  • Blood pressure
  • Serum potassium
  • Kidney function
  • Symptoms of hypotension
Safety:

ACE inhibitors and ARBs should not routinely be combined. An ACE inhibitor also requires an appropriate washout period before switching to sacubitril/valsartan because of angioedema risk.

2. Evidence-based beta-blockers

Beta-blockers reduce sympathetic stimulation of the heart and can improve outcomes in selected patients with HFrEF.

Common evidence-based options include:

  • Carvedilol
  • Metoprolol succinate
  • Bisoprolol

Important monitoring

  • Heart rate
  • Blood pressure
  • Symptoms of dizziness or hypotension
  • Worsening congestion during initiation or dose changes
Important:

Beta-blockers are generally initiated or increased when the patient is clinically compensated rather than during uncontrolled acute congestion. They should not usually be stopped suddenly.

3. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists

MRAs block aldosterone and can reduce adverse cardiac remodeling and improve outcomes in appropriate patients with HFrEF.

Common examples include:

  • Spironolactone
  • Eplerenone

Important monitoring

  • Serum potassium
  • Kidney function
  • Blood pressure
Main safety concern:

Hyperkalemia can occur, particularly in people with impaired kidney function or when other medicines that increase potassium are used.

4. SGLT-2 inhibitors

SGLT-2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin are important medicines in HFrEF.

Their heart failure benefits can occur even in people who do not have diabetes.

Important considerations

  • Kidney function
  • Volume status
  • Genital infections
  • Dehydration
  • Rare risk of diabetic ketoacidosis
Clinical pharmacy point:

A small early change in kidney function does not automatically mean that an SGLT-2 inhibitor should be stopped. The overall clinical situation and kidney function trend should be assessed.

Diuretics for fluid overload

Diuretics help remove excess sodium and water from the body. They are especially useful when heart failure causes congestion and fluid retention.

Common examples

  • Furosemide
  • Bumetanide
  • Torsemide
  • Thiazide-type diuretics in selected situations

What should be monitored?

  • Body weight
  • Edema
  • Breathlessness
  • Blood pressure
  • Kidney function
  • Sodium
  • Potassium
  • Magnesium when appropriate
Important difference:

Diuretics are mainly used to control congestion and symptoms. The four core HFrEF medicine groups are used for disease-modifying treatment.

Treatment of HFmrEF

HFmrEF occupies a middle range between HFrEF and HFpEF. Treatment may include several therapies used in HFrEF, but the strength of evidence differs between drug classes.

Important treatment options

  • SGLT-2 inhibitors
  • Diuretics when congestion is present
  • ARNI, ACE inhibitor, or ARB in selected patients
  • Beta-blocker in selected patients
  • MRA in selected patients

The patient's blood pressure, kidney function, rhythm, previous myocardial infarction, symptoms, and other indications influence the treatment decision.

Treatment of HFpEF

HFpEF is different from HFrEF. The ejection fraction may be preserved even though the heart has abnormal filling pressures and impaired relaxation.

Important treatment goals

  • Control congestion with diuretics when present
  • Control hypertension
  • Manage atrial fibrillation when present
  • Treat coronary artery disease when present
  • Manage diabetes and other comorbidities
  • Address obesity and physical inactivity when appropriate
  • Consider SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy when appropriate
Clinical point:

HFpEF treatment is highly individualized. Management of blood pressure, congestion, atrial fibrillation, coronary disease, diabetes, obesity, kidney disease, and other comorbidities is an important part of care.

Treatment of HF with improved EF

Some patients who previously had reduced ejection fraction later show an improvement in EF.

Improvement in EF does not necessarily mean that the disease has completely disappeared.

Important:

Guideline-directed HFrEF treatment should generally be continued in patients with improved EF unless a healthcare professional determines otherwise.

Acute decompensated heart failure

Acute decompensated heart failure occurs when symptoms or signs of heart failure suddenly worsen and medical treatment is required.

Common features

  • Severe shortness of breath
  • Pulmonary congestion
  • Rapid weight gain
  • Severe peripheral edema
  • Low oxygen saturation
  • Severe fatigue

Hospital treatment may include

  • IV loop diuretics for significant congestion
  • Oxygen when clinically indicated
  • Treatment of the underlying trigger
  • Management of blood pressure and perfusion
  • Correction of important electrolyte abnormalities

Treatment depends on whether the patient is congested, hypertensive, hypotensive, or in cardiogenic shock and requires hospital assessment.

When might heart failure medicines need to be reduced or changed?

Heart failure medicines require regular review. Dose adjustment depends on symptoms, blood pressure, kidney function, potassium, heart rate, fluid status, and the reason each medicine was prescribed.

Low blood pressure

Symptomatic hypotension may require assessment of volume status, other medicines, and the doses of blood-pressure-lowering medicines.

High potassium

Hyperkalemia is particularly important with MRAs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and ARNIs. Potassium, kidney function, diet, and interacting medicines should be reviewed.

Worsening kidney function

Kidney function should be interpreted in the clinical context. A change does not automatically mean that every heart failure medicine must be stopped.

Bradycardia

A slow heart rate may require review of beta-blocker dose and other medicines that reduce heart rate, especially when symptoms are present.

Dehydration

Excessive diuresis can cause dehydration, dizziness, kidney dysfunction, and electrolyte abnormalities. Diuretic treatment may therefore require reassessment when congestion has resolved.

Clinical pharmacy principle:

Do not judge a heart failure medicine only by its blood-pressure effect. Each medicine may have a specific disease-modifying or symptom- controlling role. Changes should consider the indication, benefits, adverse effects, laboratory results, and overall clinical status.

Devices and procedures

Medicines are not the only treatment options. Selected patients may benefit from procedures or devices depending on the cause and severity of heart failure.

Possible options include

  • Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD)
  • Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT)
  • Coronary revascularization
  • Valve repair or replacement
  • Left ventricular assist device (LVAD) in selected advanced cases
  • Heart transplantation in selected advanced heart failure

Device and procedural decisions require specialist assessment.

Heart failure and other diseases

Heart failure frequently occurs together with other medical conditions. These conditions can affect treatment selection and monitoring.

Important comorbidities

  • Hypertension
  • Diabetes
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Coronary artery disease
  • Obesity
  • Anemia or iron deficiency
  • Chronic lung disease

Treating these conditions appropriately can be an important part of comprehensive heart failure care.

Lifestyle and self-care

  • Take medicines exactly as prescribed
  • Monitor symptoms and weight when advised
  • Follow individualized dietary advice
  • Avoid tobacco
  • Limit excessive alcohol
  • Stay physically active when medically appropriate
  • Attend regular follow-up
  • Ask before using NSAIDs, supplements, or herbal medicines

A sudden increase in weight, swelling, fatigue, or shortness of breath may indicate worsening congestion and should be reported.

Medicines that may require caution

Some medicines can worsen fluid retention, kidney function, blood pressure, or heart failure symptoms in certain patients.

  • NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and diclofenac
  • Some medicines that cause sodium and water retention
  • Certain antiarrhythmic medicines
  • Some calcium channel blockers in HFrEF
  • Some diabetes medicines that can cause fluid retention
Always check first:

People with heart failure should tell their healthcare professional or pharmacist about all prescription medicines, OTC medicines, supplements, and herbal products.

When to seek urgent medical care

Seek urgent medical assessment for symptoms such as:

  • Severe or rapidly worsening shortness of breath
  • Severe breathing difficulty while resting
  • Chest pain or pressure
  • Fainting or severe dizziness
  • Confusion or reduced consciousness
  • Blue or grey lips or skin
  • Rapidly worsening swelling
  • Sudden severe weakness

Educational information

This page provides general educational information about heart failure. It does not replace diagnosis, individualized treatment, or advice from a qualified healthcare professional.

Heart failure treatment should be individualized according to the patient's heart failure type, ejection fraction, symptoms, blood pressure, kidney function, electrolytes, rhythm, comorbidities, and other clinical factors.

Heart failure is treatable

Modern treatment can reduce symptoms, hospitalizations, and disease progression. Treatment should be individualized.

Know the EF

Ejection fraction helps classify heart failure and can influence treatment decisions.

Medication matters

Heart failure often requires several medicines working together. Monitoring helps balance benefit and safety.
