MEDICINE INFORMATION

Kidney Disease
Medicines

Commonly encountered medicines used in chronic kidney disease, together with important considerations for kidney function, dose adjustment, monitoring, and medicine safety.

Why is kidney function important for medicines?

The kidneys remove many medicines and their metabolites from the body. When kidney function decreases, some medicines can remain in the body longer and reach higher concentrations.

Depending on the medicine, the dose may need to be reduced, the dosing interval may need to be extended, or an alternative medicine may be considered.

Clinical pharmacy point:

Kidney function should be considered whenever a medicine is prescribed, especially in patients with reduced eGFR, acute kidney injury, older age, or multiple medicines.

1. ACE Inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are commonly used in CKD, particularly when hypertension and albuminuria are present. They can reduce blood pressure and help reduce albuminuria and protect kidney function over time in appropriate patients.

Common examples

  • Enalapril
  • Lisinopril
  • Ramipril
  • Losartan
  • Valsartan
  • Irbesartan

Important monitoring

  • Serum creatinine
  • eGFR
  • Serum potassium
  • Blood pressure
Important:

ACE inhibitors and ARBs should not routinely be combined. KDIGO recommends avoiding ACE inhibitor + ARB combination therapy in CKD.

2. SGLT2 Inhibitors

SGLT2 inhibitors have become important medicines for kidney and cardiovascular protection in appropriate patients with CKD. Their benefits can extend beyond blood-glucose lowering.

Common examples

  • Dapagliflozin
  • Empagliflozin

Important considerations

  • Check kidney function and the approved indication before initiation.
  • A small initial fall in eGFR can occur after starting therapy.
  • Monitor volume status in patients at risk of dehydration.
  • Genital fungal infections can occur.
  • Rare ketoacidosis can occur.
CKD point:

KDIGO recommends SGLT2 inhibitor treatment for appropriate adults with type 2 diabetes and CKD when eGFR is at least 20 mL/min/1.73 m².

3. Diuretics

Diuretics are commonly used when CKD is associated with fluid retention, edema, hypertension, or heart failure.

Common examples

  • Furosemide
  • Torsemide
  • Hydrochlorothiazide
  • Chlorthalidone

Important monitoring

  • Blood pressure
  • Body weight and fluid status
  • Serum sodium
  • Serum potassium
  • Kidney function

Loop diuretics such as furosemide are particularly useful for significant fluid overload.

4. Statins

Cardiovascular disease is an important concern in people with CKD. Statins may be used to reduce cardiovascular risk in appropriate patients.

Common examples

  • Atorvastatin
  • Rosuvastatin
  • Simvastatin
  • Pravastatin

Safety considerations

  • Monitor for unexplained muscle pain or weakness.
  • Review potential drug interactions.
  • Consider kidney function when selecting and dosing individual statins.
Remember:

Statins are primarily used to reduce cardiovascular risk rather than to directly treat the underlying kidney disease.

5. Metformin

Metformin is commonly used for type 2 diabetes, which is an important cause and comorbidity of CKD.

Renal safety

Metformin dosing and continued use depend on kidney function. As kidney function decreases, the risk of drug accumulation and lactic acidosis becomes more important.

Important renal point:

Metformin is generally contraindicated when eGFR is below 30 mL/min/1.73 m². Dose reduction and closer monitoring may be needed at lower eGFR levels above this threshold.

6. Other Common Medicines That May Need Renal Adjustment

Many medicines used for infections, pain, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other conditions are partly or mainly eliminated by the kidneys.

Examples commonly encountered in practice

  • Metformin
  • Gabapentin
  • Pregabalin
  • Many antibiotics
  • Some antivirals
  • Digoxin
  • Some anticoagulants

The exact dose adjustment depends on the medicine, indication, kidney function, age, body size, and other patient-specific factors.

Do not use a single renal-dose rule for every medicine.

Each medicine should be checked against its current prescribing information or an appropriate dosing reference.

7. Medicines That Can Harm the Kidneys

NSAIDs

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, and celecoxib can reduce kidney blood flow and may worsen kidney function, particularly with CKD, dehydration, high doses, or prolonged use.

Important combination

The combination of an ACE inhibitor or ARB, a diuretic, and an NSAID can substantially increase the risk of acute kidney injury. This combination is sometimes called the "triple whammy."

Patient safety:

People with CKD should ask a healthcare professional before using NSAIDs, including over-the-counter pain medicines.

Renal Dose Adjustment

Dose adjustment is not automatically required for every medicine in every patient with CKD.

Possible approaches include

  • Reducing the dose
  • Increasing the dosing interval
  • Using a different medicine
  • Avoiding the medicine when risks outweigh benefits
  • Monitoring drug concentrations for selected medicines

Kidney function is commonly assessed using eGFR, while some medicines and dosing references may require creatinine clearance or another specific calculation.

Acute Kidney Injury and Medicines

Acute kidney injury (AKI) can develop rapidly and can change how medicines are eliminated.

During AKI, previously appropriate medicine doses may become unsafe. A medication review is therefore important, particularly when kidney function changes rapidly.

  • Review renally eliminated medicines.
  • Assess potentially nephrotoxic medicines.
  • Monitor creatinine and electrolytes.
  • Assess hydration and volume status.
  • Reassess doses as kidney function changes.

Important Monitoring

  • eGFR
  • Serum creatinine
  • Serum potassium
  • Serum sodium
  • Blood pressure
  • Urine albumin-creatinine ratio when appropriate
  • Fluid status and body weight
  • Adverse drug reactions

The monitoring plan depends on the medicine and the patient's stage of kidney disease.

When to Seek Medical Advice

  • Marked reduction in urine output
  • Rapidly increasing swelling
  • Severe shortness of breath
  • Persistent vomiting or severe dehydration
  • Severe weakness or confusion
  • Symptoms suggesting very high or low potassium
  • Possible medicine toxicity
Urgent:

Sudden major changes in urine output, severe breathing difficulty, confusion, or serious weakness require prompt medical assessment.

Clinical Pharmacy Focus

Renal medication review is an important clinical pharmacy responsibility. It includes checking kidney function, identifying medicines that require dose adjustment, detecting nephrotoxic medicines, assessing interactions, and monitoring treatment response.

A patient's complete medication list should include prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, vitamins, supplements, and herbal medicines.

Patient Education

  • Tell healthcare professionals that you have kidney disease.
  • Do not change medicine doses on your own.
  • Ask before using over-the-counter pain medicines.
  • Keep an updated list of all medicines and supplements.
  • Attend recommended kidney-function and laboratory monitoring.
  • Report new or worsening symptoms.
Remember:

The same medicine can require different management at different levels of kidney function. Always consider the patient's current renal function and the specific medicine.

Renal Safety

Reduced kidney function can cause some medicines to accumulate. Dose and frequency may need adjustment.

Protect Kidney Health

Appropriate medicine selection, blood pressure control, diabetes management, monitoring, and avoidance of unnecessary nephrotoxic medicines can help protect kidney health.
