CHRONIC DISEASE

Diabetes

Understanding diabetes, its symptoms, treatment, medicines, monitoring, complications, and everyday care in simple language.

What is diabetes?

Diabetes is a chronic condition in which blood glucose, also called blood sugar, becomes too high because the body does not produce enough insulin, cannot use insulin effectively, or both.

Insulin is a hormone that helps glucose move from the blood into cells where it can be used for energy.

If high blood glucose remains uncontrolled for a long time, it can damage blood vessels and nerves and affect the heart, kidneys, eyes, and feet.

Main types of diabetes

Type 1 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes occurs when the pancreas produces little or no insulin. People with type 1 diabetes require insulin for survival.

Type 2 diabetes

Type 2 diabetes occurs when the body does not use insulin effectively and, over time, may not produce enough insulin.

Type 2 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes and is associated with factors such as genetics, ageing, excess weight, and low physical activity.

Gestational diabetes

Gestational diabetes is high blood glucose first recognized during pregnancy. It requires appropriate monitoring and management during pregnancy and follow-up after delivery.

Common symptoms

Symptoms can vary between people. Type 2 diabetes may develop gradually and some people may have few or no noticeable symptoms.

  • Increased thirst
  • Frequent urination
  • Increased hunger
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Tiredness or unusual fatigue
  • Blurred vision
  • Slow healing of wounds
Important:

Symptoms alone cannot confirm diabetes. Blood glucose testing is needed for diagnosis.

Risk factors for type 2 diabetes

  • Family history of diabetes
  • Excess body weight
  • Low physical activity
  • Increasing age
  • Previous gestational diabetes
  • Some metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors

Having a risk factor does not mean that a person will definitely develop diabetes. Appropriate screening and healthy habits can help reduce risk.

Diagnosis and monitoring

Diabetes is diagnosed using blood glucose measurements. The appropriate test depends on the clinical situation.

Common tests

  • Fasting plasma glucose
  • HbA1c
  • Oral glucose tolerance test
  • Random plasma glucose in appropriate clinical situations

HbA1c provides information about average blood glucose over approximately the previous 2–3 months.

Monitoring:

Diabetes management may require regular monitoring of blood glucose, HbA1c, blood pressure, kidney function, lipids, feet, and eyes depending on the person's condition and treatment.

Possible complications

Persistently high blood glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves over time.

  • Cardiovascular disease
  • Kidney disease
  • Eye disease and vision loss
  • Nerve damage
  • Foot ulcers
  • Poor wound healing
  • Increased risk of stroke

Regular screening and appropriate treatment can help prevent or delay many diabetes-related complications.

Treatment and management

Diabetes treatment depends on the type of diabetes, blood glucose levels, other medical conditions, kidney function, cardiovascular risk, age, pregnancy status, and the medicines already being used.

Healthy living

  • Choose a balanced and nutritious diet
  • Maintain regular physical activity
  • Maintain a healthy body weight when appropriate
  • Avoid tobacco use
  • Limit excessive alcohol consumption
  • Attend recommended medical follow-up

Medicines

People with type 1 diabetes require insulin. People with type 2 diabetes may require one or more glucose-lowering medicines, depending on their individual needs.

Medicines used in type 2 diabetes can include metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, insulin, and other medicines in selected situations.

Understanding Diabetes Medicines

Diabetes medicines work in different ways. The choice of medicine should be individualized according to the type of diabetes, glucose control, kidney function, cardiovascular or kidney disease, risk of hypoglycemia, pregnancy, and other factors.

1. Metformin

Metformin mainly reduces glucose production by the liver and improves insulin sensitivity. It is widely used in type 2 diabetes.

Common adverse effects include nausea, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort, and reduced appetite.

Kidney function should be considered when metformin is prescribed because renal impairment affects its safe use.

2. SGLT-2 inhibitors

Examples include dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, and canagliflozin. They increase urinary glucose excretion.

Important considerations include genital infections, dehydration, changes in kidney function, and the rare but serious risk of diabetic ketoacidosis.

Important:

People taking an SGLT-2 inhibitor should receive appropriate advice about dehydration, acute illness, and symptoms that could indicate ketoacidosis.

3. Sulfonylureas

Examples include glibenclamide (glyburide), gliclazide, and glimepiride. They stimulate the pancreas to release insulin.

The most important adverse effect is hypoglycemia. Weight gain may also occur.

4. DPP-4 inhibitors

Examples include sitagliptin, linagliptin, and saxagliptin. They increase the activity of hormones that help regulate blood glucose after meals.

Some medicines in this group require kidney function-based dose adjustment, while others have different dosing considerations.

5. GLP-1 receptor agonists

Examples include semaglutide, dulaglutide, and liraglutide. They improve glucose-dependent insulin release, reduce glucagon secretion, and can reduce appetite and body weight.

Gastrointestinal adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea are common, particularly when treatment is started or doses are increased.

6. Insulin

Insulin replaces or supplements the body's insulin. It is essential for type 1 diabetes and may also be required in type 2 diabetes when glucose control cannot be adequately achieved with other treatment.

The major safety concern is hypoglycemia. Correct injection technique, glucose monitoring, meal planning, and individualized dose adjustment are important.

When might a diabetes medicine need to be changed?

Diabetes medicines should not be reduced, stopped, or increased without appropriate clinical assessment. A treatment change may be considered when blood glucose is not controlled, hypoglycemia occurs, kidney function changes, adverse effects become significant, pregnancy occurs, or another medical condition changes.

Hypoglycemia

Repeated or severe low blood glucose may require review of medicines that can cause hypoglycemia, particularly insulin and sulfonylureas.

Kidney function changes

Some diabetes medicines require dose adjustment or discontinuation when kidney function declines. Kidney function should therefore be reviewed regularly when clinically appropriate.

Significant gastrointestinal adverse effects

Persistent or severe gastrointestinal symptoms may require review of medicines such as metformin or GLP-1 receptor agonists.

Dehydration or acute illness

Acute illness can change glucose control and medicine safety. Some medicines may need temporary review during significant illness or dehydration.

Pregnancy

Diabetes treatment during pregnancy requires individualized assessment because medicine safety and glucose targets differ during pregnancy.

Clinical pharmacy principle:

Before changing a diabetes medicine, assess glucose readings, HbA1c when appropriate, adherence, diet, kidney function, adverse effects, hypoglycemia risk, other medicines, and important comorbidities.

Understanding hypoglycemia

Hypoglycemia means blood glucose is lower than the person's safe target range. It is particularly important in people using insulin or medicines such as sulfonylureas.

Possible symptoms

  • Sweating
  • Shaking
  • Hunger
  • Fast heartbeat
  • Dizziness
  • Confusion
  • Weakness

Severe hypoglycemia can cause seizures, unconsciousness, and other serious complications.

Emergency:

A person who is unconscious or unable to swallow safely should not be given food or drink by mouth. Emergency medical assistance should be obtained.

Diabetes and foot care

Diabetes can affect nerves and blood flow, increasing the risk of foot injuries and ulcers.

  • Check the feet regularly
  • Keep feet clean and dry
  • Protect the feet from injury
  • Wear appropriate footwear
  • Report wounds, ulcers, redness, swelling, or infection promptly

Living well with diabetes

Diabetes management is more than taking medicine. Regular physical activity, healthy eating, medication adherence, glucose monitoring when indicated, and routine follow-up all contribute to better long-term health.

When to seek urgent medical care

Urgent assessment may be needed when diabetes is accompanied by serious symptoms such as:

  • Severe difficulty breathing
  • Confusion or unusual drowsiness
  • Loss of consciousness
  • Repeated or severe hypoglycemia
  • Persistent vomiting
  • Severe dehydration
  • Rapid breathing with very high blood glucose or suspected ketoacidosis

Educational information

This page provides general health education about diabetes. It does not replace diagnosis, individualized treatment, or advice from a qualified healthcare professional.

Diabetes medicines should be selected and adjusted according to the individual's clinical condition, laboratory results, treatment goals, and current clinical guidelines.

Medicine safety

Never change insulin or diabetes medicine doses without appropriate medical guidance.

Protect long-term health

Good diabetes care includes glucose control, blood pressure management, healthy cholesterol levels, foot care, eye care, and kidney monitoring.
