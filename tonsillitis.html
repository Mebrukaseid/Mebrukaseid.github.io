Tonsillitis

Understanding tonsillitis, its common causes, symptoms, treatment approaches, home care, and medicine safety.

What is tonsillitis?

Tonsillitis is inflammation or infection of the tonsils, which are two areas of tissue located at the back of the throat.

It can occur in children, teenagers, and adults. Tonsillitis is commonly caused by viruses, but bacteria such as group A Streptococcus can also cause infection.

Common symptoms

Symptoms can vary depending on the cause and severity of the infection.

  • Sore throat
  • Pain or difficulty when swallowing
  • Red and swollen tonsils
  • Fever
  • Swollen or painful glands in the neck
  • Headache
  • Tiredness
  • Ear discomfort or earache
  • White patches or pus on the tonsils
  • Bad breath

Common causes

Viral infections

Most cases of tonsillitis are associated with viral infections. Viruses that cause common respiratory infections can also affect the tonsils.

Bacterial infections

Bacteria can also cause tonsillitis. Group A Streptococcus is an important bacterial cause of throat infection and may require specific testing and treatment.

Important:

The appearance of swollen or red tonsils alone cannot always determine whether the infection is viral or bacterial.

Viral vs bacterial tonsillitis

Viral infection

Viral tonsillitis often occurs together with symptoms such as cough, runny nose, or other symptoms of a respiratory infection.

Antibiotics do not treat viral infections.

Bacterial infection

Bacterial throat infections may cause sudden throat pain, fever, painful swallowing, swollen tonsils, and swollen lymph nodes in the neck.

A healthcare professional may use clinical assessment and, when appropriate, a throat test to help determine whether group A Streptococcus is present.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis usually begins with a medical history and examination of the throat and tonsils.

Depending on the symptoms and clinical findings, a healthcare professional may perform a throat swab or another test to determine whether a bacterial infection is present.

Testing can help avoid unnecessary antibiotic treatment when the infection is viral.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and severity of tonsillitis.

Viral tonsillitis

Viral tonsillitis usually improves on its own. Treatment mainly focuses on rest, adequate fluids, and relief of symptoms such as throat pain and fever.

Bacterial tonsillitis

If a healthcare professional determines that bacterial infection is present, an appropriate antibiotic may be prescribed.

Antibiotics should not be used simply because the tonsils are red, swollen, or have white patches. Appropriate assessment is important.

Symptom relief

Depending on the person's age, medical history, and other medicines, healthcare professionals may recommend suitable medicines to relieve pain or fever.

Antibiotic safety:

Do not use leftover antibiotics or antibiotics prescribed for another person. Unnecessary antibiotic use can cause adverse effects and contribute to antimicrobial resistance.

Home care

Mild tonsillitis can often be supported at home while the person recovers, provided there are no warning signs requiring medical attention.

Rest

Get enough rest and avoid strenuous activities while feeling unwell.

Drink enough fluids

Drink water and other suitable fluids regularly to help maintain hydration.

Warm or cool drinks

Warm drinks or cool drinks may help soothe an uncomfortable throat. Choose whichever is more comfortable.

Soft foods

Soft foods may be easier to swallow when the throat is painful.

Warm salt-water gargle

Adults and older children who can safely gargle may use warm salt water to soothe the throat. The solution should be gargled and then spat out.

Avoid smoke

Avoid smoking and exposure to secondhand smoke because smoke can further irritate the throat.

Remember:

Home care can help relieve symptoms, but it does not replace medical assessment when symptoms are severe, persistent, or worsening.

Medicine safety

  • Do not take antibiotics without appropriate medical advice.
  • Do not use another person's prescription.
  • Check the active ingredients in combination cold and flu products to avoid taking the same medicine twice.
  • Children require age-appropriate medicines and doses.
  • People who are pregnant, elderly, or living with chronic diseases should ask a healthcare professional before using medicines.
  • Tell a healthcare professional about other medicines being taken.

Prevention

Because many infections that cause tonsillitis can spread between people, good hygiene can reduce transmission.

  • Wash hands regularly with soap and water.
  • Cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing.
  • Avoid sharing cups, utensils, and personal items with someone who is sick.
  • Avoid close contact with people who have contagious respiratory infections when possible.
  • Avoid smoking and secondhand smoke.

When to seek medical care

Medical assessment is important if symptoms are severe, persistent, or getting worse.

  • Severe or rapidly worsening throat pain
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Difficulty swallowing
  • Inability to swallow fluids
  • Excessive drooling
  • Significant swelling in the mouth or throat
  • Difficulty opening the mouth
  • Severe dehydration
  • Symptoms that persist or repeatedly return
Emergency:

Difficulty breathing, inability to swallow, severe swelling of the mouth or throat, or rapidly worsening symptoms require urgent medical attention.

Educational information

This page is intended for general health education. It does not replace examination, diagnosis, or treatment by a qualified healthcare professional.

If symptoms are severe, unusual, persistent, or worsening, professional medical assessment should be sought.

Seek urgent care

Difficulty breathing, inability to swallow, severe throat swelling, or rapidly worsening symptoms require urgent medical attention.
